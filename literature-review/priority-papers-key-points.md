# Priority Papers — Key Points Extraction

This document extracts structured key points (problem/motivation, approach/method, key findings/results, limitations/threats, and research gaps) from the 31 priority "must-read" full-text papers selected for this systematic literature review on agentic AI, self-adaptive test frameworks, and autonomous quality engineering. It is intended as a working reference to support drafting the manuscript's abstract, motivation, research questions, and discussion/review sections, by consolidating concrete evidence (benchmarks, metrics, and quoted numbers) and recurring research gaps directly from the primary sources rather than from memory or secondary summaries.

## SLR001 — Software Testing with Large Language Models: Survey, Landscape, and Vision
- **Venue/Year:** IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, 2024
- **Category:** Surveys and literature reviews
- **Problem/Motivation:** Traditional software-testing techniques still struggle to generate meaningful, diverse tests with high coverage and to understand the semantics of complex software systems. The paper investigates how large language models (LLMs) can address these challenges and maps the emerging landscape of LLM-based software testing.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors conduct a systematic review using automatic searches of ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, and DBLP, manual searches of major software-engineering and artificial-intelligence venues, backward snowballing, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and quality assessment. From 14,623 initially retrieved papers, they select 102 studies and analyze them from software-testing and LLM perspectives, including testing tasks, LLMs, prompt-engineering techniques, inputs, and accompanying traditional testing techniques.
- **Key Findings/Results:** The 102 studies primarily apply LLMs to test-case preparation, program debugging, and bug repair; no practices were found for early testing activities such as test requirements and test planning. The collected studies grew from 1 paper in 2020 and 2 in 2021 to 19 in 2022 and 82 in 2023; 47% were published in software-engineering venues, 2% in artificial-intelligence venues, 5% in program-analysis or security venues, and 46% were on arXiv. About one-third of the studies used pre-training or fine-tuning, while the remainder primarily used prompt engineering; zero-shot and few-shot learning were most common, with 51 and 25 studies respectively, compared with 7 using chain-of-thought and 1 using self-consistency. The review reports substantial evaluation variability, including 2% line coverage and 1% branch coverage on the SF110 dataset, 66% API coverage for TensorFlow (2,215/3,316), and 39/40 Python bugs fixed by Codex on QuixBugs.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The paper notes the lack of widely recognized and specialized benchmarks, inconsistent evaluation datasets, and likely data leakage—for example, the Defects4J repository is included in BigQuery, a data source used by common LLMs. It also cautions that many reported results may not transfer to real-world settings because of privacy, computational, energy, and organization-specific-data constraints.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper identifies unresolved challenges in achieving high testing coverage, solving the test-oracle problem, conducting rigorous evaluations, and applying LLMs in real-world scenarios. Future work should explore early-stage, integration, acceptance, and non-functional testing; more software types; advanced prompt-engineering methods; specialized leakage-resistant benchmarks; and combinations of LLMs with traditional testing and program-analysis techniques.

## SLR002 — Large Language Models for Software Engineering: A Systematic Literature Review
- **Venue/Year:** Journal article (ACM TOSEM), 2024
- **Category:** Surveys and literature reviews
- **Problem/Motivation:** Despite rapid growth in applying LLMs to software engineering (SE) tasks, no comprehensive, systematic understanding existed of how LLMs are used, what data/tuning/evaluation strategies are applied, and which SE tasks benefit, with prior surveys narrowly scoped to single SE areas or focused only on ML/DL models.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors conduct a systematic literature review (SLR) combining manual search of six top SE venues, automated search across seven publisher platforms, and forward/backward snowballing, selecting and analyzing 395 papers (Jan 2017–Jan 2024). They organize findings around four research questions covering LLM types used, datasets/preprocessing, tuning/evaluation strategies, and the specific SE tasks LLMs are applied to.
- **Key Findings/Results:** The review identifies 85 distinct SE tasks across six activities (requirements engineering, software design, development, quality assurance, maintenance, management); software development is the most represented activity with 229 papers covering over 24 tasks, while software management is the least studied (only 3 studies). Code generation and program repair are reported as the most prevalent tasks for LLM application in development/maintenance. Codex is noted to solve "72.31%" of complex Python programming challenges, and CodeBERT compression via genetic algorithms reduced model size to "3 MB" with over "70%" latency reduction, as examples drawn from surveyed studies.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The authors acknowledge threats including possible omission of relevant papers during search despite combined manual/automated/snowballing methods, potential study selection bias in applying inclusion/exclusion criteria, and empirical knowledge bias from subjective manual analysis of 395 studies (mitigated via secondary reviewer verification).
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper's own roadmap highlights unresolved challenges in LLM deployment efficiency/model size, data dependency and benchmark contamination, generalizability across domains/languages, inadequate evaluation metrics, and interpretability/trustworthiness/ethical risks, and calls for advancing LLM-based testing/analysis and integrating programming domain knowledge into LLMs as key future directions.

## SLR003 — Large Language Models for Software Engineering: Survey and Open Problems
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE Future of Software Engineering (FoSE), 2023
- **Category:** Surveys and literature reviews
- **Problem/Motivation:** The paper surveys the rapidly emerging application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to software engineering activities and identifies the technical challenges that limit their reliability. It focuses particularly on hallucinations, nondeterminism, evaluation difficulties, and the need to ensure that generated software artefacts are correct.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors provide a broad narrative survey organized around software development activities and research domains, including requirements and design, code generation, testing, maintenance, documentation, analytics, human-computer interaction, process, and education. They also manually analyzed arXiv metadata through July 27, 2023, filtering computer-science papers and identifying LLM-related and software-engineering-related publications using title/abstract keyword queries. The survey does not report a single total number of primary papers reviewed.
- **Key Findings/Results:** The survey identifies hybrid techniques combining traditional software-engineering methods with LLMs as pivotal for reliable, efficient, and effective LLM-based software engineering. The arXiv analysis found 1,665 LLM-related computer-science preprints and 181 LLM-related papers in the software-engineering or programming-language categories in 2023 data available through July 27; more than 10% of LLM papers concerned software engineering. The paper reports that GPT-4 achieved 67% zero-shot pass@1 accuracy on HumanEval, while AlphaCode ranked in the top 54% on simulated programming competitions with more than 5,000 participants. It highlights three LLM categories—encoder-only, encoder-decoder, and decoder-only—and identifies hallucination, nondeterminism, evaluation, prompt engineering, and automated testing as crosscutting concerns.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The authors explicitly state that a survey of such a rapidly expanding area cannot aspire to be comprehensive. The arXiv keyword queries are inherently approximate, and the 2023 publication counts are underestimated because the data was accessed in July 2023.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper calls for reliable and reproducible evaluation foundations, including robust benchmarks, statistical methods that account for nondeterminism, energy and artifact reporting, and longitudinal studies of developer–LLM interaction. It also identifies hallucination mitigation, handling longer textual inputs, hybrid LLM/software-engineering processes, and underrepresented areas such as requirements engineering, design, and refactoring as open research problems.

## SLR004 — Large Language Model-Based Agents for Software Engineering: A Survey
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv, 2024
- **Category:** Surveys and literature reviews
- **Problem/Motivation:** LLM-based agents extend standalone LLMs with perception, tool use, memory, planning, and multi-agent collaboration, and are increasingly applied across software engineering tasks, but the field lacks a systematic organization of this fast-growing body of work.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors collect 124 papers via keyword searching on DBLP, snowballing, and author feedback, with two authors independently screening papers and a third arbitrating disagreements. They categorize the papers from two perspectives: the SE perspective (requirements engineering, code generation, static checking, testing, debugging, IT operations, end-to-end development/maintenance) and the agent perspective (agent framework components — planning, memory, perception, action, foundation LLM — plus multi-agent systems and human-agent collaboration).
- **Key Findings/Results:** For unit testing, agents commonly follow a generate-verify-fix pipeline targeting three goals: reducing compilation/execution errors (e.g., ChatTester, TestPilot, ChatUniTest, AgentCoder), increasing coverage (e.g., CoverUp, TELPA), and improving fault detection (e.g., MuTAP, Mokav); AutoDev is reported to achieve "99.3% test coverage on the HumanEval dataset, comparable to human-written tests." The survey also reports that "only 46.7% of the papers we surveyed have explicitly considered the efficiency of agents in SE tasks," and that "77.8%" of SWE-bench tasks can reportedly be solved by an experienced engineer within an hour, per cited prior work.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The authors acknowledge threats to validity from subjective manual paper screening (despite dual review and arbitration) possibly omitting relevant papers, and note that several surveyed strategies (e.g., multi-agent requirements engineering, knowledge-enhanced bug detection, iterative coverage improvement in unit testing, visual-input agents, specific memory formats) are backed mainly by unpublished/preprint work with weak empirical validation.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper explicitly calls for more fine-grained evaluation metrics (beyond task success rate) covering trustworthiness (robustness, security, fairness) and efficiency/cost, as well as more realistic, higher-quality benchmarks and deeper human-agent collaboration mechanisms spanning phases like architecture design, test generation, and code review.

## SLR006 — Agents in Software Engineering: Survey, Landscape, and Vision
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv, 2024
- **Category:** Surveys and literature reviews
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing software-engineering studies increasingly use LLM-based agent concepts, either explicitly or implicitly, but lack a clear definition and unified framework for such agents. The paper addresses the need to organize prior work, explain how agent technologies optimize SE tasks, and identify challenges and research opportunities.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors collected, filtered, and quality-assessed studies combining LLM-based agents with software-engineering tasks, obtaining 115 papers. They propose a framework organized around three key modules—perception, memory, and action—and classify agent behaviors including textual, visual, and auditory perception; semantic, episodic, and procedural memory; internal actions such as reasoning, retrieval, and learning; and external actions such as human/agent dialogue and digital-environment interaction.
- **Key Findings/Results:** The survey identifies 115 papers and presents a taxonomy of LLM-based agents in SE based on perception, memory, and action. Perception includes textual, visual, and auditory inputs; memory includes semantic, episodic, and procedural memory; and action includes internal reasoning, retrieval, and learning actions plus external dialogue and digital-environment actions. The paper finds that token-based textual input is dominant, while tree/graph-based, visual, and auditory modalities remain largely unexplored.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The paper mainly focuses on agents applied to software engineering and only briefly discusses the use of SE technologies to improve agent systems. It does not report a formal quantitative meta-analysis or benchmark evaluation of the surveyed approaches.
- **Research Gap Identified:** Major open problems include developing richer perception modalities and multi-role capabilities, creating an authoritative code-related knowledge base, mitigating hallucinations, and improving the computational and communication efficiency of multi-agent collaboration. The paper also calls for more sophisticated SE techniques—such as testing, tool management, and version control—to be incorporated into agent systems.

## SLR009 — AI-powered software testing tools: A systematic review and empirical assessment of their features and limitations
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv, 2024
- **Category:** Surveys and literature reviews
- **Problem/Motivation:** Conventional test automation suffers from fragile scripts, high maintenance costs, and limited adaptability to dynamic software environments. The paper addresses the lack of systematic evaluation of AI-powered testing tools' capabilities, benefits, and limitations in real-world testing scenarios.
- **Approach/Method:** The study conducted a Systematic Tool Review (STR) of 56 publicly available AI-powered testing tools, identified through Google searches and screened using inclusion criteria requiring explicit AI use and accessible documentation; general-purpose automation tools without AI features and proprietary tools without public information were excluded. The review analyzed tool documentation, demonstrations, G2 user reviews, and, where necessary, ChatGPT interactions, categorizing AI features and synthesizing qualitative evidence through qualitative coding. It also empirically evaluated Parasoft Selenic and SmartBear VisualTest on two open-source systems using Selenium test suites and manually injected mutations.
- **Key Findings/Results:** The STR found that 43 tools (77%) supported AI-powered test generation, 32 (57%) supported self-healing UI test code, 16 (29%) supported visual testing, 11 (20%) supported test-result and failure analysis, and 23 provided other AI-powered features. In the empirical study, Parasoft Selenic healed all 5 locator mutants for Spring-PetClinic but only 1 of 3 mutants for Java-Blog-Aggregator, while SmartBear VisualTest detected all 9 injected visual mutants. The tools improved test-maintenance efficiency and visual-defect detection, but limitations included difficulty handling complex UI changes, insufficiently granular visual highlighting, false positives and misclassifications, limited contextual understanding, and the continuing need for human review of AI-generated artifacts.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The STR relied partly on vendor documentation and user reviews rather than direct experimental validation of every tool, and publicly available tools may omit internal or poorly documented AI tools. The empirical study assessed only two tools and two open-source systems; its manual mutation testing and qualitative efficiency assessments may not generalize to large-scale enterprise applications.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper identifies the need for more explainable and generalizable AI models, improved handling of complex UI changes and false positives, context-aware test prioritization, and seamless CI/CD integration. It also calls for further research on AI for exploratory and security testing and on effective human–AI collaboration, since fully autonomous software testing remains a distant goal.

## SLR011 — An Empirical Evaluation of Using Large Language Models for Automated Unit Test Generation (TestPilot)
- **Venue/Year:** IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, 2024
- **Category:** Unit test generation with LLMs
- **Problem/Motivation:** Manually writing unit tests is labor-intensive, while traditional automated techniques often generate tests that are hard to understand, lack meaningful assertions, or contain spurious assertions. The paper investigates whether off-the-shelf LLMs can generate useful unit tests without fine-tuning, few-shot training data, or substantial manual effort.
- **Approach/Method:** TestPilot is an adaptive, single-LLM JavaScript test generator built on OpenAI's gpt3.5-turbo. Its pipeline dynamically identifies API functions, mines documentation comments/usage examples, prompts the LLM with each function's signature, documentation, examples, and source code to generate Mocha tests, and when a generated test fails, re-prompts with the failing test and error message to repair it.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On 25 npm packages covering 1,684 API functions, TestPilot achieved median 70.2% statement coverage and 52.8% branch coverage, versus 51.3% and 25.6% for the feedback-directed baseline Nessie. A median 61.4% of generated tests contained non-trivial assertions (median 61.6% coverage among those), the adaptive repair step fixed an average 15.6% of failing tests, and 92.8% of generated tests had ≤50% similarity to existing tests with no exact copies. Other LLMs achieved median statement/branch coverage of 68.2%/51.2% (code-cushman-002) and 54.0%/37.5% (StarCoder).
- **Limitations/Threats:** Documentation examples are matched to functions by textual name, which can misassociate examples across same-named functions, and the non-trivial-assertion metric is a low-bar proxy described as "a lower bound on the true non-trivial coverage." Results are based on only 25 npm packages and target JavaScript only, limiting generalization to other languages or proprietary code.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors plan to use static/dynamic program analysis on failing tests to understand timeout and assertion failures, obtain usage examples via users/application clients, vary LLM sampling temperature, combine TestPilot with feedback-directed techniques like Nessie, and evaluate adaptation to other (especially statically typed) languages.

## SLR012 — CodaMosa: Escaping Coverage Plateaus in Test Generation with Pre-trained Large Language Models
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE (pp. 919–931), 2023
- **Category:** Unit test generation with LLMs
- **Problem/Motivation:** Search-based software testing (SBST) tools like Pynguin frequently hit "coverage plateaus"—stalls where random mutation is extremely unlikely to produce inputs (e.g., correctly formatted strings) needed to exercise further program logic.
- **Approach/Method:** CODAMOSA runs SBST (MOSA algorithm on Pynguin) and monitors coverage progress; when coverage stalls, it identifies low-coverage callables and queries Codex to generate example test cases for them, then deserializes Codex's raw Python code into Pynguin's internal test-case representation (via "uninterpreted statements") so SBST's mutation/fitness operators can continue to refine the Codex-seeded tests.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Over 486 Python benchmarks, CODAMOSA achieves statistically significantly higher coverage on many more benchmarks (173 and 279) than it reduces coverage on (10 and 4) versus SBST-only and LLM-only baselines, respectively, with average coverage-increase magnitudes of 10% and 9% versus average decreases of −4% and −3%. In a case-study module, MOSA achieved "18.4% average total coverage" over 16 runs versus CODAMOSA's "91.8% average total coverage" (a 73.4 percentage-point increase), while a Codex-only baseline reached "64.5%" on the same module.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The paper notes that uninterpreted (Codex-derived) statements can clutter test cases and reduce readability, and that CODAMOSA does not address test oracle generation—oracles must be added separately via existing techniques (e.g., regression or mutation-testing-based oracles).
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors identify open directions including a more generic test-case representation (e.g., an augmented AST) to better capture LLM output without harming search effectiveness, integrating LLM-based hinting into other SBST algorithms (e.g., MIO) and non-evolutionary techniques like Randoop, and still-unsolved test oracle generation for LLM-seeded test suites.

## SLR013 — ChatUniTest: A Framework for LLM-Based Test Generation
- **Venue/Year:** FSE (demonstrations), 2024
- **Category:** Unit test generation with LLMs
- **Problem/Motivation:** LLM-based unit test generation tools face two key limitations: constrained context length prevents including all relevant information in prompts, and insufficient validation mechanisms cause LLMs to produce incorrect tests (compile/runtime errors) requiring costly manual repair.
- **Approach/Method:** ChatUniTest is a single-agent (non-multi-agent) LLM-based framework comprising ChatUniTest Core (prompt construction via adaptive focal context generation, generation, and a generation-validation-repair pipeline with syntactic/compile/runtime validation and rule-based plus LLM-based repair) and the ChatUniTest Toolchain (Maven and IntelliJ IDEA plugins), using gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 as the base model.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On four Java projects (Commons-Cli, Commons-Csv, an Ecommerce-microservice, Binance-connector), ChatUniTest achieved overall line coverage of "59.6%," outperforming TestSpark ("42.1%") and EvoSuite ("38.2%"); TestSpark failed on Csv due to token-limit overflow and EvoSuite failed on the Ecommerce project due to JDK incompatibility. A user study of 9 respondents found 89% used ChatUniTest to assist in writing tests, with 33% rating it "highly beneficial," and 33% actively extending the tool.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The user study sample was small (9 of 19 invited respondents), and the evaluation only compares against two baseline tools (EvoSuite, TestSpark) on four projects; TestPilot was excluded due to lack of Java support. The paper itself notes the adaptive focal context mechanism is not yet optimal and needs further enhancement.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors state plans to improve the adaptive focal context mechanism to produce optimal context per focal method, extend preparation/validation to support more programming languages, and build a broader range of benchmark implementations; user feedback also requested support for performance testing, more testing frameworks, and reliability verification in real production environments.

## SLR014 — Automated Unit Test Improvement using Large Language Models at Meta (TestGen-LLM)
- **Venue/Year:** FSE (industry track), 2024
- **Category:** Unit test generation with LLMs
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing human-written test suites are often incomplete, and naively deploying LLM-generated tests risks hallucination and unreliable code; Meta needed a fully automated way to improve existing Kotlin test classes with verifiable, non-regressing guarantees suitable for industrial-scale production deployment.
- **Approach/Method:** TestGen-LLM implements "Assured Offline LLM-Based Software Engineering" using an ensemble of two internal LLMs ("LLM1," "LLM2") to generate additional test cases for existing test classes, then passes candidates through progressively strict filters (builds, passes reliably across 5 executions to exclude flakiness, and improves coverage) before recommending fully-formed test class diffs for human code review.
- **Key Findings/Results:** In an evaluation on Instagram's Reels and Stories products, "75%" of generated test cases built correctly, "57%" passed reliably, and "25%" increased coverage. During Meta's Instagram/Facebook test-a-thons (Oct–Dec 2023), TestGen-LLM improved "11.5%"/"approximately 10%" of all 1,979 test classes to which it was applied, with "73%" of its recommendations accepted by engineers for production (compared to 50% acceptance for a prior automated repair tool); of 280 diffs submitted, 144 were accepted, 64 rejected/abandoned, 61 unreviewed, and 11 withdrawn. Per-trial success rates were low (0.04–0.05) across Facebook/Instagram platforms.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The system relies on coverage as an imperfect proxy for "improvement," and the oracle problem prevents TestGen-LLM from distinguishing a genuinely revealed bug from an incorrect test assertion, forcing it to discard any failing test. The paper notes default LLM temperature settings were mostly used, with insufficient research on application-specific probability-distribution resolution.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors explicitly flag three open problems for future work: (1) using richer improvement criteria such as mutation coverage instead of line coverage, despite its computational cost at scale; (2) developing application-aware techniques for resolving LLM probability distributions (e.g., temperature tuning) rather than relying on defaults; and (3) mitigating LLMs' tendency to "follow fashion" by replicating deprecated coding styles from training data, via prompt engineering, linter-based filters, and static analysis post-processing.

## SLR015 — CoverUp: Effective High Coverage Test Generation for Python
- **Venue/Year:** FSE, 2025
- **Category:** Unit test generation with LLMs
- **Problem/Motivation:** Manually creating tests is labor-intensive, and automatically generating high-coverage tests remains difficult because existing tools often fail to cover challenging execution paths and branches effectively.
- **Approach/Method:** CoverUp is a single-agent, iterative LLM-based pipeline using OpenAI's gpt-4o-2024-05-13 at temperature 0. It measures existing coverage with SlipCover, identifies uncovered functions/classes via Python AST analysis, prompts the LLM with targeted code excerpts and missing-coverage annotations (allowing the LLM to request additional definitions via a `get_info` tool), executes generated pytest tests, and continues the dialogue with error/coverage feedback, keeping only tests that pass and improve coverage.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On the CM benchmark (~100,000 lines, 425 Python modules), CoverUp achieved 63.8% line, 49.2% branch, and 60.3% line+branch coverage versus CodaMosa's 50.6%/28.6%/45.2% (median per-module line+branch coverage 79.6% vs 47.2%). On the MT benchmark, CoverUp reached 89.2% line/88.7% branch/89.0% line+branch coverage versus MuTAP's best of 80.8%/74.5%/77.7% (median 100% on all metrics). On the PY suite, CoverUp achieved 97.0% line, 99.2% branch, and 97.1% line+branch coverage; iterative follow-up dialogues accounted for 39.7% of successful generations (27.2% on the second prompt, 12.5% on the third).
- **Limitations/Threats:** Threats include benchmark selection, execution-environment differences and failed dependency installations (which may have reduced performance for both CoverUp and baselines), and dependence on the underlying LLM's ability to interpret prompts. Practical limitations include timeouts, incorrect static-analysis context for conditional imports, and rejecting an entire generated response when any test in it fails.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper has no separately labeled future-work section; its conclusion instead argues that simply prompting an LLM is insufficient for high-coverage test generation, and that coverage information, code context, and iterative error/coverage feedback are needed—implying further work on refining these feedback mechanisms.

## SLR016 — On the Evaluation of Large Language Models in Unit Test Generation
- **Venue/Year:** ASE, 2024
- **Category:** Unit test generation with LLMs
- **Problem/Motivation:** Manual unit-test writing is time-consuming, while automated approaches often produce tests that are hard to maintain or fail to capture code intent; prior LLM-based studies focused mainly on closed-source models with fixed prompts, leaving open-source LLMs and prompt-design effects underexplored.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors conduct an empirical study on 17 Java projects from Defects4J 2.0 using five open-source LLMs (CodeLlama-7B/13B, Phind-CodeLlama-34B, DeepSeekCoder-6.7B/33B) plus GPT-4 as reference, varying prompt description style and code features, and evaluating two in-context-learning methods (Chain-of-Thought and RAG). Generated tests are extracted with Tree-sitter, compiled, and measured for compilation success, coverage, and defect detection, compared against EvoSuite; this is a comparative single-LLM evaluation, not a multi-agent system.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Prompt design mattered: natural-language prompts for CL-7B gave 2.55% higher line coverage and 2.33% higher branch coverage than code-style prompts, while removing other focal-class methods reduced compilation success by 2.14%–14.92%. EvoSuite substantially outperformed all LLMs (78.91% line coverage vs. 40.43% for GPT-4), with 34.44%–61.78% of LLM-generated tests syntactically invalid across models. CoT and RAG had mixed/negative effects on coverage (e.g., RAG reduced CL-7B line/branch coverage by 5.57%/5.85%), and defect detection was weak: on average 87.13% of defects had no valid generated test, with only 47.28% of remaining detectable defects found (NDD: CL-7B 12, CL-13B 15, PD-34B 30, DC-7B 24, DC-33B 33, GPT-4 39).
- **Limitations/Threats:** Threats include dependence on a single benchmark (Defects4J) and the chosen LLMs, inability to evaluate all code-feature combinations due to cost, possibly suboptimal CoT/RAG adaptations, and potential data leakage (though no exact matches were found); experiments required about 3,000 A100 GPU-hours.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors propose exploring more specialized uses of CoT and RAG, self-repairing, self-consistency, and prompt-tuning methods, extending evaluation to newer benchmarks like GitBug-Java, improving post-processing to fix invalid tests, and designing mutation strategies for defect-triggering inputs.

## SLR019 — Hallucination to Consensus: Multi-agent LLM-based JUnit Test Generation with Strong Oracles
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv, 2025
- **Category:** Multi-agent and agentic test generation
- **Problem/Motivation:** Manual Java unit-test creation is labor-intensive, and existing automated techniques often optimize code coverage without producing meaningful tests or reliable oracles, while LLM-based methods frequently hallucinate incorrect assertions and require costly fine-tuning or external tools like EvoSuite.
- **Approach/Method:** CANDOR is an end-to-end, prompt-engineering-based multi-agent framework with specialized agents (Initializer, Planner, Tester, Inspector, Requirement Engineer, Panelists, Interpreters, Curator) that performs initialization, iterative coverage-guided test-prefix generation, and oracle fixing via multiple reasoning-LLM Panelists reconciled by a Curator. It uses Llama 3.1 70B as the basic LLM and DeepSeek R1 Llama-distilled 70B as the reasoning LLM, generating specification-based JUnit oracles from natural-language descriptions without fine-tuning or EvoSuite.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On HumanEvalJava, LeetCode-Medium, and LeetCode-Hard, CANDOR achieved line coverage of 0.991/0.990/0.989, branch coverage of 0.950/0.949/0.980, and mutation scores of 0.980 (2384/2443), 0.939 (574/611), and 0.937 (805/859), outperforming EvoSuite's mutation score by at least 0.049 (statistically significant, p < 1e−4). Oracle correctness was 0.910/0.930/0.894 on correct code and 0.904/0.911/0.873 on faulty code, beating TOGLL by at least 21.1 percentage points; removing the Planner agent reduced line, branch, and mutation scores by at least 0.050, 0.046, and 0.070 respectively.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Evaluation was limited to HumanEvalJava and LeetCodeJava, excluding project-level programs with user-defined or external-class dependencies, so generalization to such settings is unclear; other threats include dependence on chosen LLMs, possible benchmark data leakage, and output randomness (mitigated by repeating experiments three times).
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors plan to extend CANDOR to project-level test generation, including dedicated retrieval/dependency agents to handle external dependencies, and to explore more advanced panel-discussion strategies to further reduce hallucinations.

## SLR022 — Multi-Agent LLM Collaboration for Unit Test Generation via Human-Testing-Inspired Workflows (TESTAGENT)
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv, 2026
- **Category:** Multi-agent and agentic test generation
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing LLM-based unit test generation approaches follow rigid, procedural workflows that underutilize LLMs' autonomous reasoning and rely on rule-based context extraction that fails to capture fine-grained code dependencies and test-specific knowledge.
- **Approach/Method:** TESTAGENT is a multi-agent LLM framework with three specialized agents (requirement planner, test generator, test reviewer) that emulate human testing practices, equipped with dynamically invokable tool APIs and a test-specialized repository-level knowledge graph built via static analysis that persistently stores testing artifacts (e.g., test reports, failure analyses).
- **Key Findings/Results:** On six Java projects, TESTAGENT achieves 97.46% execution rate, 92.34% line coverage, 90.24% branch coverage, and 83.69% mutation score, outperforming LLM-based baselines on all metrics and achieving substantially higher mutation scores than search-based tools; on Python it reaches 88.85% line coverage and 78.89% branch coverage; it also detects 154 real-world bugs via non-regression tests with 92.22% precision, and is validated via industrial projects and a controlled user study.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Threats include potential data leakage from LLM pretraining (mitigated via a private industrial dataset), reliance mainly on Java with generalizability checked only via a Python re-implementation, possible LLM-selection bias (checked with DeepSeek-V3/Qwen3-30B), and results from a single run per approach due to cost, leaving non-determinism only partially addressed.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper notes consistent trends only were checked across a limited set of languages/LLMs/projects; broader validation across more languages, LLM backbones, and repeated runs to fully quantify non-determinism remains future work.

## SLR023 — SWE-agent: Agent-Computer Interfaces Enable Automated Software Engineering
- **Venue/Year:** NeurIPS, 2024
- **Category:** Agentic software engineering foundations
- **Problem/Motivation:** LM agents applying to complex software engineering tasks (e.g., real-world GitHub issue resolution) are poorly served by interfaces designed for humans (like the raw Linux shell), causing them to make avoidable mistakes such as failed file edits with no corrective feedback.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors introduce the concept of an Agent-Computer Interface (ACI) — a custom abstraction layer between an LM agent and the computer — and build SWE-agent, which uses a ReAct-style loop with a small, LM-friendly set of commands for file navigation, viewing, searching, and editing, plus guardrails (e.g., a syntax checker) that give concise, informative feedback and prevent error propagation. ACI design choices were refined through manual behavior inspection and a grid search over design variants.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Using GPT-4 Turbo, SWE-agent resolves 12.47% (reported as pass@1 12.5%) of the 2,294 SWE-bench test tasks, versus a prior non-interactive retrieval-augmented baseline of 3.8%, and achieves 87.7% pass@1 on HumanEvalFix. An ablation on SWE-bench Lite (300 instances) shows SWE-agent solves 10.7 percentage points more instances than a baseline agent restricted to the default Linux shell. The ACI also transfers to Claude 3 Opus, which solves 10.5% of benchmark tasks.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The final toolkit is deliberately small and the ACI development/case-study process was done manually rather than automated; the system is also exclusively evaluated on programmatic/code tasks, so transferability of ACI principles to other digital-work domains is untested.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors explicitly call for future work on automating ACI design (rather than manual crafting), adding further tools (web browsing, static/dynamic analysis, fault localization, fuzzing-based test generation), and investigating whether ACI principles generalize beyond software engineering to other agentic domains.

## SLR024 — Agentless: Demystifying LLM-based Software Engineering Agents
- **Venue/Year:** arXiv, 2024
- **Category:** Agentic software engineering foundations
- **Problem/Motivation:** The paper questions whether complex autonomous LLM agents (with tool use, planning, and iterative action loops) are actually necessary to resolve real-world software engineering issues, given agents' complex tool design, limited decision-planning control, and weak self-reflection.
- **Approach/Method:** AGENTLESS uses a simplistic, non-agentic three-phase pipeline—localization, repair, and patch validation—where the LLM does not decide future actions or operate complex tools, contrasting with autonomous agent-based baselines.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On SWE-bench Lite, AGENTLESS achieves the highest performance (32.00%, 96 correct fixes) among open-source approaches at low cost ($0.70 per issue); on SWE-bench Verified it solves 194/500 problems (38.80%), the second-highest among open-source methods and better than many commercial techniques; the authors also construct SWE-bench Lite-S by removing problematic issues for more rigorous comparison, and note AGENTLESS was adopted by OpenAI to showcase GPT-4o and o1 performance.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Internal threat from possible data leakage of ground-truth patches into GPT-4o's training data (cannot be fully verified since GPT-4o is closed-source); external threat that results may not generalize beyond SWE-bench Lite, though OpenAI's independent evaluation on multiple SWE-bench variants is cited as corroboration.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors plan to evaluate AGENTLESS on additional benchmarks beyond SWE-bench Lite to further address generalizability, and call for resetting the baseline/horizon for future autonomous software agent research.

## SLR025 — AutoCodeRover: Autonomous Program Improvement
- **Venue/Year:** ISSTA, 2024
- **Category:** Agentic software engineering foundations
- **Problem/Motivation:** Automatically resolving real-life GitHub issues (bug fixes and feature additions) requires more than code synthesis—trustworthy automatic program improvement—while prior approaches treat repositories as mere file collections rather than exploiting program structure.
- **Approach/Method:** AutoCodeRover combines an LLM with structured code search over program representations (AST-based classes/methods) via iterative, stratified retrieval, and incorporates spectrum-based fault localization using tests (when a test suite is available) to sharpen context before generating a patch.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On SWE-bench-lite (300 real-life GitHub issues), AutoCodeRover resolves 19% of issues, outperforming the previously reported SWE-agent; it resolved 57 issues in about 4 minutes each (pass@1) versus developers' average of 2.68 days, at a significantly lower average cost ($0.43 USD) than baselines.

- **Research Gap Identified:** Future work needs to focus on when such tools should converse with human programmers, how developer roles may shift toward "full-lifecycle" engineering, and how LLM-based autonomous software engineering can move from focusing on scale toward engendering developer trust.

## SLR026 — Large Language Models are Few-shot Testers: Exploring LLM-based General Bug Reproduction (LIBRO)
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE, 2023
- **Category:** Bug reproduction and test-based issue validation
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing automated test generation largely targets coverage or crash-based implicit oracles, leaving the semantically harder and practically important problem of generating tests that reproduce a given natural-language bug report mostly unsolved; the authors show bug-report-driven tests make up a median 28% of project test suite size.
- **Approach/Method:** LIBRO (LLM Induced Bug ReprOduction) prompts an LLM (OpenAI Codex) with the bug report to generate candidate test cases, then applies post-processing steps — clustering/ranking test outputs by features such as maximum output cluster size — to decide when generated tests are likely valid bug-reproducing tests and to rank/suggest them to developers, since the LLM itself cannot execute the buggy code.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On Defects4J, LIBRO generates a bug-reproducing test for 251 of 750 bugs (33.5%), ranks a correct reproducing test first for 149 bugs, and its validity-prediction heuristic achieves 71.4% accuracy. On a newly collected dataset of 31 post-training-cutoff bug reports (GHRB), LIBRO reproduces 32.2% (10/31) of bugs, versus 36 bugs solved by a copy-and-paste baseline in the paper's comparison figure, suggesting the approach is not merely relying on memorization.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Threats include test flakiness (<2% of generated tests) and LLM output randomness (internal validity), plus external validity concerns: results may not generalize to other languages/projects, LIBRO underperforms on projects requiring external test files or structurally atypical test infrastructure (e.g., Checkstyle, Closure), and effectiveness likely depends on the LLM having seen similar code during training.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors state they hope to "expand upon these results and explore the synergy with existing test-generation techniques to further help practitioners," indicating future work on combining LLM-based bug reproduction with traditional test generation and extending beyond Java/JUnit projects with non-standard test infrastructure.

## SLR028 — SWT-Bench: Testing and Validating Real-World Bug-Fixes with Code Agents
- **Venue/Year:** NeurIPS, 2024
- **Category:** Bug reproduction and test-based issue validation
- **Problem/Motivation:** While LLM-based code generation/repair is heavily studied, automatic test generation—especially using Code Agents to formalize natural-language issue descriptions into reproducing test cases—remains comparatively unexplored, and large-scale Python test-generation datasets are lacking.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors build SWT-BENCH, a benchmark of over 1,900 samples derived from SWE-BENCH, each with a GitHub issue, golden code patch, and golden reference tests, evaluating fail-to-pass reproduction and golden-patch coverage; they benchmark LLM prompting, LIBRO, and Code Agents (SWE-AGENT, AutoCodeRover, Aider) adapted to test generation.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Code Agents designed for code repair (notably SWE-AGENT) outperform systems designed specifically for test generation on both issue reproduction rate and coverage; an ideal ensemble of the best four methods solves 71% more samples than the best single method; filtering code fixes by self-generated passing tests doubles SWE-AGENT's precision.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Limited to Python, reducing generalizability to other languages; built from popular GitHub repositories that may not represent typical development practices and precludes a private holdout set; scope restricted to bug reproduction/coverage, not edge-case detection; risk of data contamination since most issues predate model knowledge cutoffs.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper explicitly calls for a rolling, continuously updated version of SWT-BENCH to mitigate contamination (at the cost of comparability/reproduction cost), and for specialized monitoring to address common agent errors (e.g., non-reproducing passing tests, command loops, environment execution failures, syntax errors).

- **Research Gap Identified:** Inferred — the paper focuses on task completion automation rather than software quality assurance/testing itself, leaving open how such GUI-exploration and memory-augmented LLM agent techniques could be adapted specifically for autonomous mobile app testing (e.g., bug/crash detection) rather than end-user task automation.

## SLR036 — Seeing is Believing: Vision-driven Non-crash Functional Bug Detection for Mobile Apps
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE (journal-first), 2026
- **Category:** Mobile GUI agents
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing automated GUI testing tools can detect crash bugs but largely fail to find non-crash functional bugs (e.g., wrong sort order, broken "wishlist" interactions), which the authors found make up ~61.3% of real-world GitHub issue reports, because such bugs lack reliable test oracles and require understanding operational logic across GUI page transitions.
- **Approach/Method:** The paper proposes Trident, a vision-driven multi-agent collaborative GUI testing approach built on a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM), comprising an Explorer agent (navigates the app and captures screenshots/view hierarchies), a Monitor agent (tracks exploration history and triggers detection), and a Detector agent (infers test oracles via functionality-aware chain-of-thought and flags functional bugs). It also introduces a screenshot annotation/alignment method to combine visual and textual input for the MLLM.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Evaluated on 590 non-crash bugs against 12 baselines, Trident achieves a 14%-112% boost in average recall and 108%-147% boost in average precision over the best baseline, yielding 50%-72% precision and 42%-52% recall; ablation studies confirm each module's contribution. In a real-world study on 187 Google Play apps, Trident found 43 new bugs, of which 31 were fixed and 12 confirmed by developers.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Not explicitly reported in detail beyond general statements; the paper notes current performance still leaves room for improvement and the approach is evaluated only on Android apps.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors state future work will improve Trident's performance and extend the approach beyond Android to other platforms such as web, desktop, and automotive OS for software testing.

## SLR041 — ASTER: Natural and Multi-language Unit Test Generation with LLMs
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE (Software Engineering in Practice), 2025
- **Category:** Industrial case studies
- **Problem/Motivation:** Automated unit test generation (ATG) tools exist for few programming languages, and empirically generated tests (e.g., from EvoSuite, CodaMosa) suffer poor readability/naturalness, so developers are reluctant to adopt them into regression suites without significant rewriting.
- **Approach/Method:** ASTER is a generic, multi-language LLM-based pipeline that incorporates static analysis to guide prompt construction (preprocessing for focal-method context) and postprocessing for iterative test repair and coverage augmentation, applied to Java (SE/EE, including API mocking) and Python using six different LLMs (e.g., GPT-4-turbo, Llama3-70b/8b, CodeLlama-34b, Granite-34b/8b).
- **Key Findings/Results:** ASTER is competitive with EvoSuite on Java SE coverage (within -7% to 4x-5x higher) and significantly outperforms it on Java EE (10.6%-26.4% higher coverage) while succeeding on applications EvoSuite cannot build. On Python, ASTER beats CodaMosa by +9.8%/+26.5%/+22.5% line/branch/method coverage. A 161-developer survey found >70% (Java) and 88% (Python) would add ASTER tests with minor/no changes, versus much lower acceptance for EvoSuite/CodaMosa; ASTER test names are ~15%-23% more "natural" than EvoSuite's.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The authors note limited number of evaluation runs due to cost, limitations in automated naturalness evaluation (mitigated via developer survey), and that ASTER sometimes fails to capture natural-language intent in test names (e.g., "PositiveGain" not reflected in generated code).
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper states there is still significant room to improve test naturalness/reduce test smells (e.g., ~25% of LLM-generated tests lack assertions) and suggests a fine-tuned model for test generation could close this gap further.

## SLR044 — FlakyLens: re-evaluation of LLM flaky-test classification
- **Venue/Year:** OOPSLA, 2025
- **Category:** CI failure triage and flaky-test classification
- **Problem/Motivation:** Prior LLM-based flaky-test classifiers (e.g., Flakify, FlakyCat) reported very high accuracy, but the authors argue this was an over-estimate due to flawed experimental design (data leakage) and unrealistic, imbalanced datasets that misrepresent real-world flaky/non-flaky test distributions.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors build FlakeBench, a realistic dataset (280 flaky, 8294 non-flaky tests) with correct per-project train/test splits, then propose FlakyLens, a new fine-tuning strategy for CodeBERT tailored to this imbalanced setting; they further use Integrated Gradients to extract important code tokens and apply adversarial perturbations to probe model robustness.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Fixing experimental design dropped prior SOTA F1 from 81.82% to 56.62%; FlakyLens improves this to 65.79% F1 (9.17pp higher than corrected SOTA, 13.79pp higher than corrected FlakyCat). FlakyLens correctly classifies 8468/8574 tests and outperforms the best zero-shot pre-trained LLM baseline (14.86% F1) by 50.93pp. Perturbing important tokens reduces F1 by up to 18.37pp versus only 8.70pp for least-important tokens, showing reliance on syntactic cues rather than semantics.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Stated threats include external validity (Java-only open-source projects, generalizability to other languages untested), not comparing against larger proprietary LLMs (e.g., GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7) due to cost, a fixed set of flaky-test categories that excludes causes like environment/network/GUI flakiness, and reliance solely on test code (missing flakiness rooted outside test code).
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors call for further research into more robust training methodologies so classifiers understand semantic context rather than category-specific tokens, and propose future work expanding FlakeBench to more languages and flakiness categories.

## SLR049 — FlakyGuard: Automatically Fixing Flaky Tests at Industry Scale
- **Venue/Year:** ASE, 2025
- **Category:** CI failure triage and flaky-test classification
- **Problem/Motivation:** LLM-based flaky test repair tools like FlakyDoctor fail in industrial settings due to the "context problem" — providing either too little production-code context (missing root cause) or too much context (overwhelming the LLM in 100K+ LOC services), especially given Go's table-driven testing idioms common at Uber.
- **Approach/Method:** FlakyGuard treats the codebase as a dynamic call graph (built from test execution traces) and uses an LLM-guided, iterative selective graph exploration (rather than fixed-depth BFS/DFS or ego-graphs) to retrieve only relevant context at arbitrary depth, combined with AST-based test simplification and patch transplantation for table-driven tests.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Deployed over six months at Uber, FlakyGuard repairs 47.6% of reproducible flaky tests with 51.8% of fixes accepted by developers, outperforming state-of-the-art approaches (FlakyDoctor, Agentless, AutoCodeRover, RepoGraph) by at least 22% in repair success rate; 100% of surveyed developers found its root-cause explanations useful.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The authors note LLM non-determinism as a threat to evaluation validity, mitigated via retries (M=3 context attempts × P=2 reasoning paths × N=3 fix attempts = 18 repair opportunities per test); the evaluation targets Go specifically, though the authors argue the approach generalizes with minimal language-specific adaptation (confirmed informally on Java).
- **Research Gap Identified:** Not explicitly stated as future work beyond continued deployment; the paper implies (inferred) that broader multi-language validation beyond Go/Java and handling of non-call-graph-related flakiness sources remain open.

## SLR050 — AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents
- **Venue/Year:** NeurIPS (Datasets and Benchmarks), 2024
- **Category:** Security of coding and testing agents
- **Problem/Motivation:** LLM agents that call tools over untrusted data are vulnerable to (indirect) prompt injection attacks that can hijack agent behavior, and existing benchmarks evaluate either single function calls or multi-turn tasks without explicit, evolving adversarial attacks/defenses.
- **Approach/Method:** AgentDojo is an extensible, dynamic benchmarking framework (not a static test suite) populated with 97 realistic tasks (e.g., email, e-banking, travel booking) and 629 security test cases, evaluating agents via formal utility checks on environment state rather than LLM-simulated environments, and supporting pluggable attacks and defenses (e.g., prompt injection detectors, tool filtering, prompt repetition).
- **Key Findings/Results:** State-of-the-art LLMs solve less than 66% of tasks even without attacks; existing prompt injection attacks succeed in less than 25% of cases against the best agents, dropping to 8% attack success rate with a secondary attack detector defense, and to 7.5% with a tool-filtering defense (though this fails on 17% of test cases where attack and task tools overlap); most defenses incur 15-20% utility loss under attack.
- **Limitations/Threats:** The authors acknowledge the pre-deployed attacks/defenses are general-purpose and not tailored to specific tasks/scenarios, and isolation-based defenses can fail when tool sequences can't be planned in advance or when multi-task/no-context-reset scenarios are used (not yet covered by AgentDojo).
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper explicitly calls for more sophisticated adaptive attacks/defenses (e.g., isolated LLM planners), automating task/utility specification to scale the benchmark, and notes that significant breakthroughs in LLMs' ability to distinguish instructions from data will likely be needed to thwart stronger adaptive attacks.

## G1 — Make LLM a Testing Expert: Bringing Human-like Interaction to Mobile GUI Testing via Functionality-aware Decisions (GPTDroid)
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE, 2024
- **Category:** GUI, mobile and web testing agents
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing DL/RL-based automated mobile GUI testing tools suffer from low testing coverage, poor generalization to new/unseen app states, and heavy dependence on large amounts of training data.
- **Approach/Method:** GPTDroid reformulates mobile GUI testing as an iterative Q&A dialogue with an LLM (e.g., ChatGPT): it extracts GUI page semantics (via decompilation/view hierarchy) into a prompt, asks the LLM to generate executable test scripts/actions, executes them on the app, and feeds back results. A functionality-aware memory prompting mechanism lets the LLM retain long-term testing context across the whole exploration process.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Evaluated on 93 Google Play apps against random/rule-based, model-based, and learning-based baselines; GPTDroid achieves 75% average activity coverage and 66% average code coverage, 32% (0.75 vs. 0.57) higher activity coverage than the best baseline, and detects 95 bugs (31% more, 95 vs. 66) at a faster rate (saving 35%, 17 vs. 26 minutes). It also found 53 new bugs on Google Play, with 35 confirmed/fixed; ablation shows GUI context and functionality memory modules are both critical (removing GUI context drops activity coverage to 0.17).
- **Limitations/Threats:** The paper notes challenges with widgets/inputs lacking meaningful text/resource-id and apps requiring specific operations (e.g., database connections, drag-to-location) that are difficult to automate; no formal threats-to-validity section is highlighted beyond these uncovered cases.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors state future work includes exploring more advanced prompt engineering and fine-tuning open-source LLMs specifically for GUI testing to further improve performance.

## O1 — TOGA: A Neural Method for Test Oracle Generation
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE, 2022
- **Category:** Test oracles and assertions
- **Problem/Motivation:** Synthesizing functional test oracles (the intended, not implemented, behavior of code) is challenging, especially for units with ambiguous/missing documentation or no implementation, limiting automated test generation's bug-finding power.
- **Approach/Method:** TOGA is a unified transformer-based neural approach with two components (exception oracle classifier and assertion oracle ranker over template-generated candidates) that infers both exceptional and assertion oracles from a focal method's context and test prefix; it is integrated with EvoSuite-generated test prefixes for end-to-end bug finding.
- **Key Findings/Results:** TOGA improves assertion oracle accuracy by 33% over prior approaches, reaching 96% accuracy on the in-vocabulary ATLAS* test set and 69% overall (vs. 63%/62% baselines); exceptional oracle inference reaches 86% accuracy (F1 .39 vs. coin baseline's .15). Integrated with EvoSuite on Defects4J, it finds 57 real-world bugs, including 30 not found by any other method in the evaluation, and 5 of these are expected-exception bugs undetected by any other tool.
- **Limitations/Threats:** TOGA's restricted grammar/vocabulary means it cannot express all oracles (54% of missed bug-finding assertions had out-of-vocabulary values, 8.5% fell outside the grammar); it also depends heavily on EvoSuite's test-prefix structure and is trained on developer-written tests that differ in distribution from EvoSuite-generated ones (in-vocab accuracy drops to 31% on EvoSuite tests vs. 96% on ATLAS*). Threats to validity include dataset bias (ATLAS/Methods2Test/Defects4J) and EvoSuite's limited bug-reaching coverage (120 of 835 Defects4J bugs).
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors suggest training on EvoSuite-generated (test, oracle) pairs instead of developer-written datasets to better match end-to-end automated testing distributions, and integrating mutation-analysis tools (e.g., PIT) to generalize beyond EvoSuite-specific test-prefix formats.

## O2 — Neural-Based Test Oracle Generation: A Large-scale Evaluation and Lessons Learned
- **Venue/Year:** ESEC/FSE, 2023
- **Category:** Test oracles and assertions
- **Problem/Motivation:** The paper investigates whether TOGA's promising reported effectiveness for neural test oracle generation actually holds in broader, real-world settings, since prior evaluation was limited in scale and methodology.
- **Approach/Method:** The authors conduct external, extended, and conceptual replication studies of TOGA on a new large-scale dataset of 25 real-world Java systems (223.5K test cases, 51K injected faults via mutation testing), measuring fault-detection improvement, oracle-type classification accuracy, precision/false-positive rate, and mutation-kill contribution relative to EvoSuite and JUnit implicit oracles.
- **Key Findings/Results:** TOGA reproduces the original 57-bug detection count, but only 19 of these are attributable to its own oracles (38 are caught by EvoSuite's implicit "No Exception" failures). TOGA misclassifies oracle type 24% of the time, is not confident enough to generate an assertion 62% of the time it classifies correctly, and over 47% of generated assertions are false positives; true-positive TOGA assertions increase fault detection by only 0.3% (105 of 51,385 mutants killed exclusively by TOGA), and JUnit's implicit oracle alone detects over 50% of bugs.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Threats to validity include possible shifted input distribution from the 25 new applications, reliance on EvoSuite-only test prefixes (a limitation inherited from TOGA), and potential bugs in the authors' own replication tooling (JUnit/PIT), mitigated via sanity testing and repeated runs.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper explicitly calls for the research community to develop techniques that reduce neural oracle generation's high false-positive rate for industrial adoption, and recommends future evaluations use implicit/JUnit oracles as baselines, report precision alongside recall, and use mutation testing rather than fixed-vs-buggy test-prefix reuse.

## P1 — A Multi-Agent Approach for REST API Testing with Semantic Graphs and LLM-Driven Inputs (AutoRestTest)
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE, 2025
- **Category:** API testing
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing black-box REST API testing tools treat test elements (APIs, parameters, values) in isolation, leading to lower code/operation coverage and weaker fault detection.
- **Approach/Method:** AutoRestTest is a dependency-embedded multi-agent approach combining multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) with four specialized agents (API, dependency, parameter, value), a semantic property dependency graph (SPDG) to reduce the dependency search space via operation similarity, and LLMs for domain-specific value generation.

Ablation shows removing any component (SPDG, LLM, or MARL) drops coverage by 10.9–13.9 percentage points.

- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors plan future work on dynamic adaptation of testing strategies, optimizing performance/scalability (e.g., fine-tuning LLMs), developing more sophisticated fault-classification approaches, and studying how specification completeness/accuracy affects performance.

## Z1 — Fuzz4All: Universal Fuzzing with Large Language Models
- **Venue/Year:** ICSE, 2024
- **Category:** LLM-based fuzzing
- **Problem/Motivation:** Existing fuzzers are typically language-specific and limited to predefined input features, making them hard to generalize across different programming/formal languages or to reveal bugs tied to new language features.
- **Approach/Method:** Fuzz4All is the first "universal" fuzzer that uses LLMs as an input generation and mutation engine; it introduces an autoprompting technique to construct fuzzing-suited LLM prompts and an LLM-powered iterative fuzzing loop that updates prompts with code examples and generation strategies to produce diverse inputs.
- **Key Findings/Results:** Evaluated on nine systems under test spanning six languages (C, C++, Go, SMT2, Java, Python), Fuzz4All improves coverage over the previous state-of-the-art by 36.8% on average and identifies 98 bugs in systems like GCC, Clang, Z3, CVC5, OpenJDK, and Qiskit, with 64 confirmed by developers as previously unknown.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Threats to validity include implementation correctness (mitigated via code review and 5 repeated 24-hour runs for statistical significance), reliance on a fixed LLM checkpoint (StarCoder) that may degrade with data-shift over time, and the distillation LLM's potential to "hallucinate" inaccurate information.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The authors note hallucination in the LLM-driven pipeline is a common limitation they hope to address in future work, and more broadly note that autoprompting can mitigate but not eliminate the risk of outdated model knowledge.

## Z2 — Large Language Models Are Zero-Shot Fuzzers: Fuzzing Deep-Learning Libraries via Large Language Models (TitanFuzz)
- **Venue/Year:** ISSTA, 2023
- **Category:** LLM-based fuzzing
- **Problem/Motivation:** Traditional fuzzing is largely ineffective for deep-learning (DL) libraries because valid input programs must simultaneously satisfy host-language syntax/semantics and complex DL API input/shape (tensor) constraints.
- **Approach/Method:** TitanFuzz is the first approach to directly leverage LLMs for fuzzing DL libraries in a zero-shot manner: it uses a generative LLM (Codex) to produce high-quality seed programs via prompt engineering, then an infilling LLM (InCoder) to mutate seeds through an evolutionary fuzzing algorithm, exploiting LLMs' implicit learning of DL API usage patterns from training corpora.
- **Key Findings/Results:** On TensorFlow and PyTorch, TitanFuzz covers 2215 and 1329 library APIs respectively, a 91.11% and 24.09% increase over the best baseline (DeepREL); it achieves 30.38%/50.84% higher code coverage than state-of-the-art fuzzers on TensorFlow/PyTorch (20.98% PyTorch / 39.97% TensorFlow line coverage overall), and detects 65 bugs with 41 confirmed as previously unknown.
- **Limitations/Threats:** Internal validity threat from TitanFuzz's own implementation (mitigated via code review/testing); external validity threat from relying on only two DL libraries (PyTorch, TensorFlow) as benchmarks, though these are the two most popular.
- **Research Gap Identified:** The paper states it hopes to "stimulate more work in this promising direction of LLMs for fuzzing" generally, implying (inferred) that extending zero-shot LLM fuzzing to other specialized, constraint-heavy domains beyond DL libraries remains open future work.

## Cross-Paper Synthesis

### Common Themes
- **Multi-agent collaboration outperforms single-agent/monolithic LLM pipelines for test generation and oracle quality**, with specialized roles (planner, generator, reviewer/inspector, curator) reducing hallucinated assertions and improving mutation scores and oracle correctness (SLR019, SLR022, SLR036, P1, SLR004, SLR006).
- **Coverage-guided iterative feedback loops (execute → measure → re-prompt) consistently outperform one-shot LLM prompting**, whether seeding search-based testing (SLR012), repairing failing tests (SLR011, SLR013, SLR015), or exploring call graphs for flaky-test repair (SLR049).
- **The test-oracle problem remains the central unsolved bottleneck**: neural oracle generation (O1) looked highly accurate in its original evaluation but was shown to have >47% false positives and negligible unique fault-detection contribution under rigorous independent replication (O2); LLM-based oracle hallucination is addressed partially via multi-agent consensus (SLR019) but is flagged as unresolved in multiple surveys (SLR001, SLR012, SLR014).
- **Simple, well-engineered non-agentic pipelines can rival or beat complex autonomous agents at much lower cost**, challenging assumptions that more agentic autonomy is always better (SLR024 vs. SLR023/SLR025).
- **Benchmark and evaluation validity is systematically overstated without rigorous, leakage-free, and realistically imbalanced experimental design**, a pattern repeated across oracle generation (O1 vs. O2) and flaky-test classification (SLR044), and flagged broadly as a methodological risk in the LLM4SE surveys (SLR001, SLR002, SLR003, SLR016).
- **LLM-based test/bug-reproduction and repair tools consistently struggle with context management**—too little production-code context misses root causes, too much overwhelms the model—addressed via retrieval/graph-based context selection (SLR049), static-analysis-guided prompting (SLR041, SLR013, SLR015), and structured code search (SLR025).
- **GUI/mobile testing agents (vision-driven or LLM-dialogue-based) substantially outperform classical random/model-based/RL exploration on coverage and bug detection**, especially for non-crash functional bugs that traditional oracle-less tools miss (G1, SLR033, SLR036).
- **Security of autonomous coding/testing agents is an emerging, largely separate concern from functional capability**: even state-of-the-art agents fail many ordinary tasks and remain vulnerable to prompt injection, with defenses trading off utility for safety (SLR050).
- **Industrial deployments reveal persistent gaps between research benchmarks and production needs**—naturalness/readability of generated tests (SLR041), assured non-regression guarantees (SLR014), and six-month-scale reliability at companies like Meta and Uber (SLR014, SLR049)—showing that raw coverage or accuracy metrics are insufficient proxies for developer-perceived usefulness.

### Consolidated Research Gaps (candidate RQs/motivation material)
- **Reliable, non-hallucinating test oracle generation remains unsolved**, especially generalizing beyond EvoSuite-style test prefixes and restricted grammars/vocabularies (O1, O2, SLR001, SLR012, SLR014, SLR019).
- **Lack of rigorous, leakage-free, realistically distributed benchmarks and evaluation protocols** for LLM-based testing/agent research, with several "SOTA" results shown to be inflated under correct experimental design (O2, SLR044, SLR002, SLR016, SLR028).
- **Limited understanding of when agentic complexity (multi-step planning, tool use, multi-agent debate) is actually necessary** versus when simpler pipelines suffice, and how to automate interface/ACI design rather than hand-crafting it (SLR023, SLR024, SLR025).
- **Generalization across languages, project scales, and domains is largely untested**: most tools are validated on Java/Python/JavaScript and small-to-medium open-source or single-company codebases, leaving industrial-scale, multi-language, and repository-level (cross-file dependency) generalization open (SLR013, SLR019, SLR022, SLR041, SLR044, SLR049).
- **Fault/defect-detection effectiveness, not just coverage, is under-evaluated**: many unit-test generation studies optimize line/branch coverage while reporting weak or unmeasured real fault-detection ability (SLR011, SLR015, SLR016, SLR001).
- **Context management for LLM agents operating on large codebases is unresolved**—balancing insufficient vs. overwhelming context for root-cause analysis and repair (SLR049, SLR025, SLR004).
- **Security and robustness of autonomous coding/testing agents against adversarial or untrusted inputs is nascent**, with no testing-specific adversarial benchmarks yet and general-purpose agents still vulnerable to prompt injection even with defenses (SLR050).
- **Developer trust, readability, and human-agent collaboration are underexplored relative to raw technical performance**, including naturalness of generated tests, explainability of agent decisions, and the appropriate degree of human oversight/review (SLR009, SLR041, SLR025, SLR003).
- **Non-crash, non-functional, and early-lifecycle testing activities (requirements-based test planning, non-functional testing, GUI functional-bug oracles) remain largely unaddressed** compared to crash/exception-based or coverage-based testing (SLR001, SLR036, G1).
- **Efficiency, cost, and energy considerations of agentic pipelines are rarely systematically measured**, despite being critical for industrial adoption at scale (SLR004, SLR014, SLR049).

### Suggested Motivation Paragraph (draft, 150-200 words)
The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) in software testing has given rise to a new generation of agentic, self-adaptive, and autonomous quality-engineering techniques, ranging from multi-agent unit-test generation frameworks that reconcile hallucinated oracles through panelist consensus, to vision-driven GUI agents that detect non-crash functional bugs invisible to crash-based testing, to self-healing locators and flaky-test triage systems deployed at industrial scale. Yet this progress is uneven and often methodologically fragile: independent replications have shown that headline accuracy claims for neural test-oracle generation and flaky-test classifiers can be substantially overstated once data leakage and unrealistic class balance are corrected, while several studies suggest that simple non-agentic pipelines can match complex autonomous agents at a fraction of the cost. Persistent challenges—unreliable test oracles, limited generalization beyond Java/Python and small-to-medium benchmarks, unmeasured fault-detection effectiveness beneath coverage metrics, unresolved context-management strategies for large codebases, and emerging security vulnerabilities such as prompt injection against coding agents—indicate that the field lacks a systematic, cross-cutting synthesis of what agentic AI testing techniques reliably deliver versus where genuine autonomy remains aspirational. This review addresses that gap.

### Suggested Abstract Draft (150-250 words)
Context: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly embedded in autonomous and self-adaptive software-testing pipelines, spanning unit-test generation, GUI exploration, bug reproduction, flaky-test triage, fuzzing, and REST API testing, promising to automate activities long considered to require human judgment. Problem: Despite rapid growth, the literature is fragmented across techniques, benchmarks, and claims of effectiveness, with several influential results later shown to be overstated once rigorously re-evaluated, making it difficult to assess the genuine state of agentic AI in quality engineering. Method: This systematic literature review analyzes 31 priority full-text studies, complementing a broader corpus, spanning eight technique categories—surveys, unit-test generation, multi-agent/agentic test generation, agentic software-engineering foundations, bug reproduction/validation, mobile and GUI testing agents, test oracles, CI/flaky-test triage, API testing, LLM-based fuzzing, and agent security—to synthesize approaches, reported results, and limitations. Findings: Multi-agent architectures and coverage-guided feedback loops consistently improve test quality over single-shot prompting, but the test-oracle problem, cross-language/cross-scale generalization, fault-detection validity, context management for large codebases, and agent security remain substantially unresolved; several widely cited "state-of-the-art" results do not hold under corrected, leakage-free evaluation. Conclusion: Advancing truly autonomous software testing requires rigorous, contamination-resistant benchmarks, fault-detection-centric (not coverage-centric) evaluation, and dedicated attention to agent robustness, security, and developer trust alongside continued architectural innovation.
