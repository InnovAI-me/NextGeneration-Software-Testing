\subsection{Overview of Selected Studies}
\label{subsec:corpus-overview}

The working corpus assembled for this review comprises 149 primary
studies identified through systematic queries and screening across digital
libraries and preprints.
Of the 149 studies, 120 have been assigned to
one of 18 substantive thematic categories through iterative open
coding against titles, abstracts, and full texts;
the remaining 29 studies represent foundational baseline works
analyzed as general background rather than forced into a narrow category.
The 18 categories, in descending order of frequency, are: Security of coding
and testing agents (15), Human factors: trust and acceptance (15),
Flaky tests and self-healing tests (11), Test oracles and assertions
(10), Surveys and literature reviews (10), CI failure triage and
flaky-test classification (9), Unit test generation with LLMs (8),
GUI, mobile and web testing agents (6), Industrial case studies (5),
Multi-agent and agentic test generation (5), Mobile GUI agents (4),
Benchmarks and data contamination (4), Self-adaptive systems and
runtime testing (4), Bug reproduction and test-based issue validation
(3), LLM-based fuzzing (3), API testing (3), Agentic software
engineering foundations (3), and Mutation testing with LLMs (2). This
distribution is itself an empirical finding: more than a fifth of the
categorized corpus (30 of 120 studies, 25\%) concerns risk rather than
capability -- security of coding/testing agents and human trust/
acceptance are jointly the two largest categories, each larger than
the combined unit-test-generation and multi-agent-generation
literature that much of the field still treats as its core subject
matter. This asymmetry motivates the emphasis given to RQ4 below and
to the dedicated QE-safety synthesis in Section~\ref{sec:qe-safety}.

The corpus's venue distribution reflects the rapid pace of development in
agentic testing. Peer-reviewed software engineering venues (such as ICSE,
ASE, and \emph{IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering}) represent core
anchors, supplemented by timely preprints and foundational technical reports,
consistent with the post-2023 publication surge analyzed next.

Figure~\ref{fig:pub-trend} and the year-of-publication distribution show the field's explosive and
very recent growth. Across the 149 tracked studies (one record has no
resolvable year), publication counts by year are: 2018 (1), 2019 (1),

2026 (39, as of this review's cut-off date and therefore a partial
year). Only nine studies in the entire corpus (6\%) predate 2023; the
remaining 94\% were published in the four years from 2023 onward, and
2024 alone accounts for nearly a third of the corpus (46 of 149).
This step change coincides with the maturation of instruction-tuned,
tool-using large language models (GPT-4-class and successor models)
into agentic software-engineering pipelines, and it mirrors the
trajectory documented by the field's own survey literature
\cite{slr0012024,slr0022024,slr0032023,slr0042024,slr0052024,slr0062024}.
Two implications follow for how this review's evidence should be
read. First, the literature is young: most empirical claims rest on
single-study results from 2024--2026 rather than on independently
replicated, converging evidence, so effect sizes reported below should
be read as early indicators rather than settled findings. Second, the
corpus is still accelerating -- 2026 already contributes 39 studies
despite covering less than a full calendar year at the time of
writing -- which argues for treating this review's taxonomy and
synthesis as a snapshot of a fast-moving target rather than a
terminal classification, consistent with the phased, re-runnable
protocol defined for this review.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:category-dist}, the categories
cluster naturally into four super-groups that track the test
life cycle framing used throughout this review (design $\rightarrow$
generation $\rightarrow$ execution $\rightarrow$ oracle $\rightarrow$
maintenance/triage), plus a cross-cutting risk dimension. The
\emph{generation-centric} cluster -- unit test generation with LLMs
(8), multi-agent and agentic test generation (5), bug reproduction and
test-based issue validation (3), LLM-based fuzzing (3), and mutation
testing with LLMs (2), 21 studies in total -- addresses how tests and
test inputs are synthesized. The \emph{interface-and-execution}
cluster -- GUI, mobile and web testing agents (6), mobile GUI agents
(4), and API testing (3), 13 studies -- addresses how agents drive a
system under test once inputs exist. The \emph{judgment} cluster --
test oracles and assertions (10) -- addresses how pass/fail decisions
are made, a category whose size (the fourth largest in the taxonomy)
reflects the oracle problem's centrality to autonomous testing. The
\emph{operational} cluster -- flaky tests and self-healing tests (11),
CI failure triage and flaky-test classification (9), and self-adaptive
systems and runtime testing (4), 24 studies -- addresses what happens
to tests after they are written, the maintenance and triage end of the
life cycle. Framing, evidence, and context studies -- surveys and
literature reviews (10), industrial case studies (5), and agentic
software-engineering foundations (3) -- total 18 studies. Finally, the
\emph{risk} cluster -- security of coding and testing agents (15) and
human factors: trust and acceptance (15) -- is, taken together, the
single largest thematic grouping in the taxonomy at 30 studies,
alongside a small but consequential benchmarks-and-contamination
category (4) that qualifies how much of the generation-centric and
judgment evidence can be trusted at face value. The remainder of this
section answers RQ1--RQ4 against this structure.

\subsection{RQ1: Taxonomy of Techniques}
\label{subsec:rq1-taxonomy}

The generation-centric literature is the most mature sub-area, with
the earliest techniques following a straightforward pattern: condition
an LLM on the focal method (and increasingly, retrieved context) and
have it emit unit-test code directly. TestPilot
\cite{slr0112024} demonstrated this for TypeScript libraries using
only API documentation and signatures; CodaMosa \cite{slr0122023}
instead hybridizes LLM generation with search-based testing
(Pynguin), invoking the model specifically when coverage plateaus,
showing that LLMs are most valuable as a targeted escape mechanism
for classical techniques rather than a wholesale replacement.
ChatUniTest \cite{slr0132024} and Meta's TestGen-LLM
\cite{slr0142024} push toward industrial deployment: TestGen-LLM
reports that LLM-generated tests were accepted into Meta's production
codebase after automated build/pass/coverage filtering, evidence that
the generate-then-filter pattern can survive contact with a real CI
pipeline. CoverUp \cite{slr0152025} iterates generation against live
coverage feedback to target high line/branch coverage for Python, and
a cluster of evaluation-focused studies
\cite{slr0162024,slr0172024,slr0182024} characterizes how sensitive
unit-test-generation quality is to prompt design, model choice, and
target language -- recurring findings are that compilable-but-vacuous
assertions and prompt brittleness remain open problems even as raw
compilability rates improve.

A second, architecturally distinct generation paradigm delegates test
construction to multiple cooperating LLM agents rather than a single
pass. Multi-agent JUnit generation with explicit consensus mechanisms
\cite{slr0192025,xu2025candor} assigns separate roles (e.g., a
generator and one or more critics) that must agree before a test is
accepted, reporting double-digit correctness gains over single-model
baselines; MUATC \cite{slr0202025} and HPCAgentTester
\cite{slr0212025} extend multi-agent utilization to coverage
augmentation and HPC-specific code respectively, while TESTAGENT
\cite{slr0222026} explicitly models its workflow on how human testers
decompose a task. These multi-agent systems sit alongside, and often
build directly on, general-purpose agentic software-engineering
frameworks such as SWE-agent's agent-computer interface
\cite{slr0232024}, Agentless's deliberately non-agentic alternative
\cite{slr0242024}, and AutoCodeRover's program-repair-oriented
autonomy \cite{slr0252024} -- the agentic-testing literature is, in
this sense, a specialization of a broader agentic-software-engineering
foundation (3 studies), not an isolated sub-field. A closely related
generation task, automated bug reproduction, uses LLMs to synthesize
failure-triggering tests from bug reports: LIBRO \cite{slr0262023}
established the "few-shot tester" framing, a follow-up study
\cite{slr0272023} re-evaluated it across 15 LLMs to test
generalizability, and SWT-Bench \cite{slr0282024} turns bug-fix
validation itself into a benchmark for code agents, directly bridging
the generation and benchmarking clusters discussed under RQ3.

Interface-driving agents form the second major technique family. On
mobile, GPTDroid \cite{liu2024} and its antecedent zero-shot GUI
exploration work \cite{liu2023} recast GUI testing as a conversational,
function-aware interaction between the LLM and the app under test;
DroidAgent \cite{yoon2023} frames exploration as an intent-driven
autonomous agent rather than a scripted monkey tester. A second wave
targets specifically the perception problem -- AutoDroid
\cite{slr0332024} grounds task automation in Android's accessibility
tree, while AUITestAgent \cite{slr0342024} and the vision-driven
agents VisionDroid \cite{slr0352024} and a related non-crash
functional-bug detector \cite{slr0362026} instead condition on
screenshots via multimodal LLMs, trading accessibility-tree fragility
for vision-model cost and latency. On the web, NaviQAte
\cite{shahbandeh2024} and AutoE2E \cite{alian2024} target
functionality-guided navigation and end-to-end test generation
respectively, and WebTestPilot \cite{g62026} generalizes the
vision-driven paradigm to test web applications directly against
natural-language specifications using a vision-language-model agent.
API-level interface testing is comparatively nascent (3 studies):
AutoRestTest \cite{stennett2025,same2025} combines a multi-agent
architecture with semantic operation-dependency graphs and
LLM-driven input synthesis for REST APIs, and RESTGPT
\cite{kim2024} similarly targets REST API test improvement via LLM
assistance -- both report fault-detection gains over prior
search-based REST testers, though neither has yet been independently
replicated outside its originating group.

Input-generation at the fuzzing end of the spectrum has also been
reframed around LLMs. Fuzz4All \cite{xia2024} positions an LLM as a
universal input generator spanning multiple target languages/systems
within one framework, in contrast to the traditionally
grammar-specific nature of fuzzers. TitanFuzz \cite{deng2023} and
FuzzGPT \cite{deng2023b} target deep-learning library fuzzing
specifically, with FuzzGPT explicitly optimizing for edge-case,
historical-bug-triggering inputs rather than raw coverage -- a
fault-centric framing this review returns to under RQ3. Mutation
testing, the smallest technique category (2 studies), uses LLMs
generatively but for a different purpose: synthesizing mutants rather
than tests. Wang et al.'s comprehensive mutation-testing study
\cite{wang2024} and LLMorpheus \cite{tip2024} both report that
LLM-generated mutants are more diverse and more often
semantically-higher-order than traditional operator-based mutants,
though at a higher per-mutant generation cost.

Finally, the judgment problem -- test oracles and assertions, the
fourth-largest category (10 studies) -- spans neural oracle synthesis
and the meta-question of whether an LLM oracle can be trusted at all.
TOGA \cite{dinella2022} and the large-scale evaluation that followed
it \cite{hossain2023} established neural oracle generation as
distinct from test-input generation; TOGLL \cite{o32024} and
AugmenTest \cite{o42025} extend this toward stronger, LLM-driven
oracle correctness guarantees. A second, more recent sub-thread --
largely surfaced through the QE-safety synthesis -- interrogates the
reliability of LLM judges themselves: Bodicoat et al.
\cite{bodicoat2026oracle} and Molina and Gorla
\cite{molina2024oracleautomation} study oracle generation and
automation specifically through an LLM-as-code-generator lens, while
Wataoka et al. \cite{wataoka2024selfpreference} and Panickssery et
al. \cite{panickssery2024evaluators} show that an LLM judge's ability
to recognize its own outputs is causally linked to how favorably it
scores them, and Huang et al. \cite{huang2023selfcorrect} and Olausson
et al. \cite{olausson2023selfrepair} show LLMs cannot reliably
self-correct reasoning or self-repair code without an external
signal. Taken together, RQ1's technique landscape is broad and
increasingly agentic, but the judgment layer that must certify any of
it is, by this review's own category counts, nearly as heavily
studied as generation itself -- and, as RQ4 discusses, for reasons
that are more cautionary than celebratory.

\subsection{RQ2: Automation and Autonomy Levels}
\label{subsec:rq2-autonomy}

Classifying studies by where in the test life cycle autonomy is
exercised -- design, generation, execution, oracle, or
maintenance -- shows that automation has advanced furthest at the
generation and execution stages (analyzed under RQ1 in
Section~\ref{subsec:rq1-taxonomy}) and is least mature, yet
most operationally consequential, at the maintenance stage: keeping a
test suite usable once it has started failing. Two adjacent
maintenance categories dominate this cluster by study count: flaky
tests and self-healing tests (11 studies) and CI failure triage and
flaky-test classification (9 studies), together 20 of the corpus's 120
categorized studies. Flaky-test-focused work spans empirical
characterization and automated repair. FlakyFix \cite{fatima2025}
predicts flaky-test fix categories and attempts automated repair;
self-healing locator approaches for Robot Framework
\cite{h2nd,h32026} use LLMs to re-identify UI elements whose
selectors have drifted. A larger empirical sub-thread quantifies how
hard flakiness detection actually is: an evaluation of flaky-failure
classifiers across 230,439 test failures \cite{alshammari2024flakyclassifiers}
reports that failure-deduplication logic is "entirely ineffective" on
some projects; CANNIER \cite{parry2023cannier} combines rerunning with
machine-learning classification to cut detection cost by an order of
magnitude, at the price of an empirical recall ceiling (even 10,000
reruns catch only roughly two-thirds of flaky tests); systemic
flakiness \cite{parry2025systemic} shows flaky failures co-occur
rather than behaving as independent events; a Microsoft study of the
flaky-test lifecycle \cite{lam2020lifecycle} finds that developer- or
tool-claimed "fixes" frequently do not change a test's actual failure
behavior; and Meta's Probabilistic Flakiness Score
\cite{meta2020pfs} explicitly rejects a binary flaky/not-flaky label
in favor of a continuously monitored reliability score, so that a
sudden post-fix increase in failure rate stays visible rather than
being silently absorbed by a "healed" label. Self-healing-locator
tooling itself -- a zero-cost DOM-accessibility-tree approach
\cite{joseph2026zerocost}, LLM-based Robot Framework locator repair
\cite{rohamo2024robotframework}, and an empirical comparison against
the Healenium framework \cite{ijam2025healenium} -- reports large
reductions in locator-failure rate and near-complete pass-rate
recovery, but, as discussed under RQ4, measures success purely as
"the test passes again," not as "the test still verifies the same
behavior."

CI failure triage extends this maintenance automation from individual
flaky tests to whole-pipeline diagnosis. LogSage \cite{slr0422025}
and a related industrial log-analysis study \cite{slr0432026} apply
LLMs to CI/CD failure detection and remediation with production
validation; FlakyLens \cite{slr0442025}, an empirical exploration of
LLM-based flaky-test conversation \cite{slr0452026}, fine-tuned and
few-shot flaky classifiers \cite{slr0462025}, a C++-specific
flakiness identifier \cite{slr0472024}, the neuro-symbolic NeuroFlake
framework \cite{slr0482026}, and FlakyGuard's industry-scale automatic
fixing \cite{slr0492025} together show flaky-test classification
converging on hybrid neuro-symbolic and fine-tuned architectures
rather than zero-shot prompting alone, while an industrial study of
detected-versus-undetected flaky failures in real CI pipelines
\cite{leinen2026detectedundetected} frames the detector/healer
boundary symmetrically -- every detection mechanism implies a
corresponding undetected-failure population that may include real
regressions misclassified as noise. Beyond test-suite maintenance,
self-adaptive systems and runtime testing (4 studies) addresses a
related but architecturally distinct autonomy problem: testing
systems that themselves reconfigure at runtime. An architectural
runtime-model testing scheme \cite{hnsel2018}, the "perpetual
assurances" framing of continuous self-adaptive-system verification
\cite{weyns2019}, a review of how self-adaptive systems are evaluated
\cite{wohlrab2021}, and a survey of runtime testing for dynamically
adaptable and distributed systems \cite{sa42021} collectively suggest
that assurance for self-adaptive systems is treated as a continuous,
never-finished activity rather than a pre-release gate -- a framing
this review's taxonomy adopts for autonomous QE more broadly, in which
maintenance-stage autonomy (self-healing, triage) is not a lesser
concern than generation-stage autonomy but its necessary complement.

\subsection{RQ3: Empirical Evidence}
\label{subsec:rq3-evidence}

Reported effectiveness evidence is strongest, and most convergent,
for coverage-centric metrics and weakest for fault-detection and
reproducibility. Industrial case studies (5 studies) provide the
clearest signal that LLM-based test generation survives deployment:
Ericsson's program-analysis-augmented fine-tuned LLM pipeline
\cite{slr0372025}, a study of LLM integration into industrial test
maintenance \cite{slr0382024}, an account of unit-test generation
challenges and lessons learned in industrial environments
\cite{slr0392025}, CATGen's focus on practical reliability of
LLM-based unit-test generation \cite{slr0402026}, and ASTER's
natural, multi-language unit-test generation \cite{slr0412025} report
consistent gains in compilability and coverage relative to
unassisted baselines, but none report a standardized, cross-study
fault-detection metric -- each uses its own internal benchmark, which
limits comparability across the five studies.

This comparability problem is compounded by a distinct and smaller but
consequential category: benchmarks and data contamination (4 studies),
which directly qualifies how much of the generation-centric evidence
summarized under RQ1 can be taken at face value. TestGenEval
\cite{slr0292025} proposes a real-world unit-test-generation and
test-completion benchmark explicitly designed to be harder to
memorize than function-signature-only benchmarks; a study asking
whether LLMs are "memorizing bug benchmarks"
\cite{slr0302025} finds evidence consistent with contamination on
at least some widely used bug-fixing datasets; the GitHub Recent Bugs
Dataset \cite{slr0312023} is explicitly constructed to postdate common
training cutoffs as a mitigation; and a study of inter-dataset code
duplication and data leakage \cite{slr0322024} shows that leakage is
not confined to a single benchmark but propagates across supposedly
independent ones via shared source repositories. The practical
implication for RQ1's generation-centric results is that
headline compilability/coverage numbers reported on long-standing
public benchmarks (e.g., Defects4J-derived corpora) should be
discounted relative to numbers reported on benchmarks explicitly
designed, like TestGenEval and the GitHub Recent Bugs Dataset, to
resist memorization. A related, narrower fault-centric evaluation
thread appears in the fuzzing literature under RQ1: FuzzGPT
\cite{deng2023b} is unusual in this corpus for explicitly optimizing
for historical-bug-triggering inputs rather than raw code coverage,
illustrating that fault-detection-centric evaluation, while rarer
than coverage-centric evaluation across the corpus as a whole, is
achievable when a study deliberately designs for it. Across RQ3 as a
whole, then, the empirical record supports confident claims about
compilability and coverage improvement from agentic/LLM-based testing
techniques, but only cautious, benchmark-qualified claims about actual
fault-detection improvement, and essentially no cross-study
meta-analysis of effect sizes given how recent and heterogeneously
evaluated this literature is (see Section~\ref{subsec:pub-trends} and Section~\ref{subsec:rq2-autonomy}).

\subsection{RQ4: Open Challenges}
\label{subsec:rq4-challenges}

The corpus's two largest categories -- security of coding and testing
agents and human factors: trust and acceptance, 15 studies each, 30 in
total -- are also its most forward-looking: both concern whether
autonomous testing and coding agents can be trusted with the
authority this review's taxonomy shows they increasingly hold (RQ1,
RQ2). On security, four benchmark/attack studies
\cite{zhan2024injecagent,debenedetti2024agentdojo,guo2024redcode,she2025queryipi}
establish that indirect prompt injection is a real, quantifiable
threat rather than a theoretical one: InjecAgent reports a ReAct-style
GPT-4 agent hijacked in roughly 24\% of cases by maliciously crafted
content embedded in the data it processes, nearly doubling under
reinforced attacker prompts \cite{zhan2024injecagent}; AgentDojo
confirms that injection attacks reliably break some, but not all,
tested security properties across 629 test cases
\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}; RedCode's 4,050 test cases show agents
inconsistently refuse risky operations, and more capable models can
produce more sophisticated malicious code once jailbroken -- a
capability/safety inversion -- \cite{guo2024redcode}; and QueryIPI
shows that payloads hidden in invariant context such as tool
descriptions -- rather than per-query triggers -- achieve up to 87\%
success and transfer to real IDE-integrated coding agents, meaning an
attacker need not predict a developer's query, only poison the
tool/plugin ecosystem a testing agent consults
\cite{she2025queryipi}. Complementary industrial-scale studies show
risk is not confined to adversarial settings: a systematic analysis of
security debt in autonomous coding agents \cite{sakib2026trustverify}
finds that 38.9\% of agent-authored pull requests in a large-scale
dataset contain security smells (82.3\% of those are supply-chain
issues, and 99.6\% of critical smells are hardcoded credentials), with
existing human review catching only around 19\% of leaked credentials
before merge, and a study of insecure behaviors arising even during
benign, non-adversarial developer-aid tasks \cite{kozak2025developeraid}
reports 21\% of agent trajectories are insecure during ordinary setup
tasks, dominated by information-exposure weaknesses -- directly
undermining "a human reviews the agent's output" as a sufficient
safety story for AI-generated tests and code. A
corresponding defense literature is emerging in parallel: a lightweight
repository-borne-injection benchmark and defense study
\cite{slr0512026}, a study of memory-file prompt injection against
coding agents \cite{slr0522026}, and an analysis of sandbox-escape
"trust handoff" flaws in AI coding agents \cite{slr0532026} identify
new attack surfaces, while VibeGuard's pre-publish security gate
\cite{xie2026vibeguard}, ROSA's metamorphic-fuzzing backdoor detector
\cite{kokkonis2025rosa}, Progent's SMT-verified least-privilege policy
enforcement \cite{shi2025progent}, IsolateGPT's OS-style execution
isolation at under 30\% overhead for most queries
\cite{wu2024isolategpt}, and SkillShield's prompt-space, weight-free
defense matching a dedicated 8B guard classifier's blocking rate at a
0.14\% false-refusal rate \cite{wu2026skillshield} each mitigate a
specific slice of the threat surface -- VibeGuard's pre-publish gate
separately reports 100\% recall and 89.47\% precision on
packaging/secret-exposure failures missed by conventional static
analysis, and ROSA's metamorphic fuzzing oracle detects planted
backdoors in roughly 90 minutes without reverse engineering -- but, as
AgentDojo's own framing makes explicit, attacks and defenses remain
incomplete and co-evolving rather than settled
\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}.

On the human-factors side, the 15-study category spans developer
perception of LLM-assisted testing tooling, standards for testing AI
systems, and the governance of highly autonomous agents. Developer
acceptance work shows a consistent concern with trust and transparency
rather than raw output quality: studies of how developers perceive
amplified tests \cite{slr0542024,slr0552023,slr0592024}, a mapping of
the broader LLM-in-software-engineering trust landscape
\cite{slr0562025}, LLM-generated verifications for manual tests
\cite{slr0572024}, and PyTestGuard's IDE-level support for developers
consuming LLM-generated unit tests \cite{slr0582025} together indicate
that acceptance of agentic testing tools depends less on measured
accuracy than on whether developers can inspect, explain, and override
what the tool produced. At the standards and governance level,
ISO/IEC TR 29119-11 \cite{iso2020tr29119} and ISO/IEC TS 42119-2
\cite{iso2025ts42119} supply risk-based guidance for testing AI as the
system under test but, as this review's taxonomy makes visible by
grouping them with trust/acceptance rather than with a dedicated
standards category, do not address the inverse and increasingly urgent
scenario this review's own taxonomy foregrounds -- AI acting as the
tester or quality gatekeeper. Feng et al.'s five-level autonomy
taxonomy \cite{feng2025autonomylevels}, Treude et al.'s terms-of-service
analysis showing accountability is currently settled by vendor
contract rather than engineering standard \cite{treude2026accountable},
Cheng et al.'s three-pillar transparency/accountability/
trustworthiness model \cite{cheng2026threepillar}, IBM's CHI workshop
study of trust and reliance in evolving human-AI workflows
\cite{ibm2024trew}, two independent overreliance studies
\cite{passi2025overreliance,ibrahim2025overreliance}, and Kiran's
enterprise human-AI collaboration model for scalable testing
\cite{kiran2026humanai} collectively show that as agents move beyond a
"collaborator" role toward "approver" or "observer" autonomy, a human
is no longer meaningfully in the quality-sign-off loop, yet no
standard, contract, or regulation yet assigns traceable accountability
for that condition.

This security-and-trust evidence motivated a dedicated \emph{Quality
Engineering Safety} literature thread -- security of testing/coding
agents, test-oracle independence and the AI-testing-AI circularity
problem, self-healing/flaky-test stability, and governance/
accountability for Level~4--5 autonomy -- synthesized from 36
additional sources (Section~\ref{sec:qe-safety}) specifically to
deepen RQ4. These sources encompass specialized security benchmarks,
adversarial attack analyses, and emerging governance guidelines
(including relevant international standards and industrial white papers)
that provide critical context for autonomous testing risks.
Beyond the security evidence summarized under RQ4 in this subsection,
this thread's synthesis surfaces two further findings that cut across RQ1--RQ3.
First, the oracle-independence
problem documented under RQ1 (LLM judges favoring low-perplexity,
self-recognized outputs \cite{wataoka2024selfpreference,panickssery2024evaluators})
means that consensus among multiple same-family LLM agents, as used
by CANDOR-style multi-agent oracle generation \cite{xu2025candor}, can
still produce correlated false confidence rather than genuine
independence when the underlying models share representational blind
spots or overlapping pretraining data -- a risk this review's
synthesis terms a provenance gap, since no paper in the corpus
measures or discloses training-data overlap between a code-generating
agent and the LLM judge that scores its output. Second, the RQ2
maintenance-automation evidence (self-healing locator recovery rates
of 40--60\% fewer locator failures and near-complete pass-rate
recovery \cite{joseph2026zerocost,rohamo2024robotframework,ijam2025healenium})
is, across every surveyed self-healing study, validated only against
cosmetic DOM or attribute changes with no underlying functional
change -- no paper in this corpus evaluates whether a self-healing
test, once repaired, still detects a deliberately injected real
regression, meaning current self-healing evidence demonstrates
maintenance-cost reduction but not preserved fault-detection capacity.
A third, unifying observation emerges from reading all four QE-safety
sub-threads together rather than in isolation: each independently
converges on the same structural weakness, namely that the
"healed/passed/approved" signal currently relied on in practice is a
single, gameable proxy that is not independently verified -- whether
that signal is a self-healing locator's pass rate, an LLM oracle's
verdict, a code agent's benign-looking tool call, or a human
reviewer's sign-off on an agent-authored pull request. None of the
mechanisms surveyed in this subsection (policy/privilege control,
execution isolation, multi-agent consensus, probabilistic flakiness scoring)
individually closes this gap; each substitutes a different single
proxy for the one it replaces rather than requiring a second,
independently sourced signal before the first is trusted. Taken
together with the security findings discussed in this subsection,
RQ4's open-challenges picture is one in which autonomous testing's
generation and execution capabilities (RQ1) have advanced faster than
the mechanisms needed to trust their outputs (oracle independence),
secure their operation (prompt injection, sandbox escape, supply-chain contamination),
verify their maintenance claims (self-healing without regression masking),
or govern their growing decision authority (accountability for
Level~4--5 autonomy) -- a gap this review carries forward into the
discussion and the ten-problem research roadmap in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} and the conclusion.
