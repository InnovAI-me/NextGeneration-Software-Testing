The rapid uptake of large language models (LLMs) and autonomous
agents in software engineering has produced, in little more than
three years, a dense secondary literature of its own. At least ten
surveys, systematic literature reviews (SLRs), or structured mapping
studies have attempted to summarize some facet of AI-assisted
testing or AI-assisted software engineering between 2023 and 2025.
This density is itself informative: it signals that the underlying
primary literature is moving faster than any single review can track,
and that different reviewing teams have drawn the scope boundary in
different places -- by SE activity, by granularity (unit versus
system level), by artifact type (LLM versus agent), or by whether the
LLM is treated as subject or as tool. This section surveys that
landscape in three groups -- test-generation-centric reviews,
general-purpose LLM-for-SE reviews, and agentic/autonomous-SE reviews
-- and then states explicitly, group by group, what each omits and
how the present review's scope, time window, and taxonomy are
constructed to close those omissions.

\subsection{Surveys of AI-Based and LLM-Based Test Generation}
\label{subsec:prior-surveys-gen}

The review closest in subject matter to the present work is
\emph{Software Testing with Large Language
Models: Survey, Landscape, and Vision}~\cite{slr0012024}, which systematically analyzes
102 primary studies on LLM applications across the testing
life cycle -- test generation, program repair, oracle construction,
and debugging -- and proposes a landscape taxonomy organized around
testing tasks. It is the single most comparable prior SLR in terms of
method (systematic search and classification over LLM-for-testing
primary studies) and remains a valuable baseline for the pre-agentic
state of the field. However, by its own scope statement it treats
LLMs strictly as point tools invoked for individual testing tasks,
and it does not examine self-adaptive execution loops, multi-step
agent architectures, or questions of operational autonomy -- the
central organizing axis of the present review. A complementary,
tool-centric survey~\cite{slr0092024}
takes a different angle: it systematically reviews
AI-powered commercial and open-source software testing tools and
empirically assesses their advertised features, most notably
self-healing locators, against observed limitations. This is one of
the few prior works to engage with self-healing test automation at
all, but its unit of analysis is the tool/vendor rather than the
primary research literature, and it explicitly flags that independent
empirical evidence for vendor self-healing claims is sparse -- a gap
the present review addresses directly by synthesizing the flaky-test
and self-healing primary-study literature (Section~\ref{sec:results})
rather than vendor documentation.

Three further reviews narrow the lens specifically to unit testing.
One SLR~\cite{slr0082025} systematically reviews LLM-based
unit test generation current to March 2025, and another~\cite{slr0072025}
reviews achievements, challenges, and opportunities in LLM-based unit
test generation with particular attention to fault-detection
effectiveness and the absence of standardized benchmarks. Both are
methodologically careful SLRs, and both explicitly restrict their
corpus to the unit level -- neither engages with system-level,
end-to-end, or agentic test generation, and neither examines
governance, security, or human-factors concerns around deploying such
tools in production pipelines. Their research-gap statements note,
respectively, that no standard benchmarks exist for deciding when
unit-level generation is adequate, and that fault-detection
effectiveness of generated unit tests is still weak relative to
human-written suites. These are important, well-evidenced findings
that the present review incorporates (Section~\ref{sec:results}), but
by construction they cannot speak to multi-agent test generation,
GUI/mobile test agents, CI-triage agents, or any of the
higher-autonomy categories that make up roughly a third of this
review's 149-study corpus. Finally, a further survey~\cite{slr0102024} offers a
software-engineering perspective on testing \emph{of} LLMs --
benchmarks, evaluation practice, and tooling for assessing LLM
quality as a software artifact -- which is a necessary but distinct
problem from testing \emph{with} LLMs. We treat it as background
context on evaluation methodology (as in
Section~\ref{sec:background}) rather than as a competing review of
the same object of study, since its inclusion criteria target a
different research question entirely.

Taken together, this cluster of five reviews establishes that
LLM-assisted test generation, chiefly at the unit level, is a mature
enough sub-area to support multiple independent systematic syntheses.
None of them, however, adopts autonomy or agency as an organizing
construct, none extends its corpus past early-to-mid 2025, and only
one~\cite{slr0092024} touches self-healing test maintenance, doing
so from a commercial-tooling rather than a primary-research
perspective. It is also worth noting the publication maturity of this
cluster: only one of the five has appeared in a peer-reviewed journal
venue~\cite{slr0012024}; the remainder are arXiv preprints or
workshop submissions at the time of writing, which does not diminish
their evidentiary value but does mean that their findings have not
yet undergone the editorial revision cycle this review itself is
subject to. We treat all ten comparison surveys -- peer-reviewed and
preprint alike -- as candidate secondary evidence rather than as
settled fact, and cross-check their headline claims (such as reported
fault-detection rates or self-healing success rates) against the
underlying primary studies wherever both are present in this review's
corpus, rather than citing survey-level conclusions without
verification.

\subsection{General LLM-for-Software-Engineering Surveys}
\label{subsec:prior-surveys-llmse}

A second cluster of reviews treats testing as one activity among many
within the broader LLM-for-SE research program. The most
comprehensive of these, \emph{Large Language Models for Software
Engineering: A Systematic Literature Review}~\cite{slr0022024}, applies a systematic search and
classification protocol across the full SE life cycle -- requirements,
design, coding, testing, maintenance -- with testing appearing as a
single task category among many rather than as the unit of analysis.
Its corpus closes in January 2024, which by its own account predates
most of the agent-based testing literature that this review's corpus
(extending through 2026) is able to capture; indeed, agentic
architectures for SE had barely been formalized as a research topic
at that point. A second, earlier contribution,
\emph{Large Language Models for
Software Engineering: Survey and Open Problems}~\cite{slr0032023}, is explicitly a
position paper presented at the ICSE Future of Software Engineering
track rather than a systematic review: it does not apply a
reproducible search-and-screening protocol, but it is useful
background because it was among the first works to articulate the
``plausible-but-wrong'' failure mode of LLM output as a first-class
SE risk and to call for hybrid SE-plus-LLM methods combining
classical static/dynamic analysis with generative models. That call
is a direct antecedent of several hybrid oracle- and
verification-focused primary studies surveyed in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, but the position paper itself contains no
systematic testing-specific analysis, no inclusion/exclusion
criteria, and no taxonomy comparable to the eighteen-category scheme
developed here.

Because both of these broader surveys necessarily allocate only a
fraction of their synthesis effort to testing -- testing being one of
perhaps six or seven SE activities each covers -- neither can offer
the granularity this review provides on, for instance, oracle
circularity in LLM-as-judge evaluation, the empirical stability of
self-healing locators under UI drift, or the security posture of
testing agents specifically (as opposed to coding agents generally).
Their value to the present review is instead as adjacent evidence on
general LLM behavior (hallucination rates, prompt sensitivity, context
window limitations) that recurs as a root cause across the
testing-specific primary studies synthesized in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. It is also worth noting that these two
broader surveys are, by design, snapshots of a fast-moving field taken
roughly a year apart from one another (January 2024 and late 2023,
respectively); neither could have incorporated the subsequent wave of
agent-framework releases and benchmark suites that occupy a
料share of this review's own corpus, and both explicitly
acknowledge, in their respective discussion sections, that their
conclusions about LLM reliability in SE tasks should be read as
time-bound rather than as settled characterizations of the technology.

\subsection{Surveys of Agentic and Autonomous Software Engineering}
\label{subsec:prior-surveys-agentic}

A third, more recent cluster addresses agentic software engineering
directly, but, symmetrically to the previous cluster, treats testing
as one downstream application of agent architectures rather than as
the primary object of study. \emph{Large Language
Model-Based Agents for Software Engineering: A Survey}~\cite{slr0042024} systematically
surveys agent designs -- planning, memory, tool use, multi-agent
coordination -- across the SE life cycle, but testing agents receive
only shallow treatment relative to coding and program-repair agents,
and the review does not analyze testing-specific failure modes such
as flaky self-healing or oracle circularity. \emph{From LLMs to LLM-based Agents for Software Engineering: A
Survey of Current, Challenges and Future}~\cite{slr0052024} similarly surveys the
transition from single-turn LLM usage to multi-step agentic workflows
in SE and is notable for an explicit methodological caveat: it finds
no unified standard or benchmark exists for deciding what qualifies as
an ``LLM-based agent'' at all, a definitional instability that
complicates any attempt at systematic inclusion/exclusion screening
in this space. \emph{Agents in Software
Engineering: Survey, Landscape, and Vision}~\cite{slr0062024} reaches the same
conclusion independently -- that the field lacks a settled definition
of ``agent'' in the SE context -- and proposes its own landscape
taxonomy to partially address this, but, again, with testing agents as
a minor branch of a much larger tree spanning coding, requirements,
and DevOps agents.

These three surveys collectively establish an important
methodological precondition for this review: the absence of a
standard operational definition of ``agent'' in software engineering
is a recognized, unresolved problem in the secondary literature
itself, not an idiosyncrasy of this review's screening process. We
address it directly in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} by adopting an
explicit operational definition of autonomy (bounded by observable
properties -- planning, environment perception, tool invocation, and
closed-loop correction -- rather than by self-description in primary
studies) and by using that definition consistently across all
eighteen taxonomy categories. This operational stance also underlies
the second of this review's four research questions (autonomy level
and architecture: single-agent, multi-agent, or tool-augmented LLM),
which has no counterpart research question in any of the three
agentic-SE surveys discussed here, since none of them screens primary
studies specifically for testing-related autonomy properties. None of
the three agentic-SE surveys,
moreover, engages with the quality-engineering-specific hazards that
autonomy introduces into testing specifically: an agent that can
autonomously rewrite its own locators, regenerate its own assertions,
or judge its own test outputs raises oracle-circularity and
self-correction-reliability concerns (Sections~\ref{sec:results},
\ref{sec:discussion}) that have no analogue in, say, an autonomous
code-repair agent evaluated against a fixed, human-authored test
suite. These surveys also predate or only partially overlap with the
agent-security literature on indirect prompt injection, tool-privilege
escalation, and benign-operation insecurity in coding/testing agents
that this review treats as a dedicated taxonomy category.

\subsection{Positioning of This Review}
\label{subsec:positioning-review}

Reading these ten prior surveys together reveals a consistent
structural pattern rather than ten unrelated gaps: each review draws
its scope boundary along one of two axes -- either restricting the
\emph{activity} (testing only, and within that, often unit testing
only) while remaining agnostic to autonomy, or restricting the
\emph{technology} (agents in general) while remaining agnostic to
which SE activity the agent performs. None combines a testing-specific
activity scope with an autonomy-specific technology scope, and none
treats quality-engineering safety -- the reliability, security, and
governance properties required before autonomous testing artifacts
can be trusted in production -- as a first-class organizing category
rather than an incidental observation. This review is positioned
precisely at that intersection, and its distinguishing contributions
relative to the surveys discussed in Sections~\ref{subsec:prior-surveys-gen}--\ref{subsec:prior-surveys-agentic} can be summarized as follows.

First, \textbf{scope}: this review's unit of analysis is testing
activities performed with varying degrees of autonomy, spanning
generation, execution, maintenance, oracle construction, and triage,
rather than either ``testing performed by any LLM''
(\cite{slr0012024}) or ``any SE activity performed by an agent''
(\cite{slr0042024,slr0052024,slr0062024}). This single difference in
unit of analysis is what allows the present review to place a unit
test generated by a single prompt invocation, a self-healing locator
repaired by a runtime agent, and a fully autonomous multi-agent test
suite on one continuum rather than in three disconnected literatures.
Second,
\textbf{temporal coverage}: the corpus spans publications from 2018 --
predating the LLM era, to capture the self-adaptive and runtime
testing literature that forms a conceptual precursor to today's
testing agents -- through 2026, giving an eight-year window that
subsumes and extends every prior survey's cutoff, several of which
close in 2023 or early-to-mid 2024 (\cite{slr0022024,slr0032023})
before most agentic testing work existed. Third,
\textbf{breadth and granularity of taxonomy}: the review classifies
149 primary studies into eighteen categories spanning not only
generation tasks (unit test generation, GUI/mobile/API test agents,
multi-agent test generation, LLM-based fuzzing, mutation testing) but
also maintenance and triage tasks (flaky-test and self-healing-test
research, CI failure triage and flaky-test classification),
foundational and evaluative concerns (test oracles and assertions,
agentic SE foundations, benchmarks and data contamination,
self-adaptive systems and runtime testing), and -- most distinctively
relative to prior surveys -- safety and governance concerns
(security of coding and testing agents, human factors of trust and
acceptance, industrial case studies), none of which receives dedicated
taxonomic treatment in any of the ten prior reviews. Fourth, and
central to the review's contribution, is the explicit
\textbf{quality-engineering safety lens}: where one review~\cite{slr0092024}
notes in passing that independent evidence for self-healing claims is
limited and two others~\cite{slr0052024,slr0062024} note in passing that
``agent'' lacks a settled definition, this review treats oracle
circularity (an agent generating and then judging its own test
assertions), self-healing stability under distributional UI or API
drift, agent-security exposure (indirect prompt injection and
tool-privilege escalation specific to testing agents), and governance
and accountability requirements for autonomous QA pipelines as
primary synthesis targets with their own evidence base, cross-cutting
discussion (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), and open-problem agenda
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}), rather than as footnoted caveats.

Methodologically, this review also differs from the two narrative or
position-paper-style contributions in the comparison set
(\cite{slr0032023,slr0102024}) in following a reproducible
protocol -- multi-database systematic search, explicit
inclusion/exclusion criteria, and PRISMA-style study-flow reporting
\cite{kitchenham2007,page2021prisma} -- and from the narrower unit-
test SLRs (\cite{slr0072025,slr0082025}) in deliberately not
restricting inclusion to a single granularity level, precisely because
the central research question concerns how autonomy properties
recur, or fail to recur consistently, across granularities from a
single generated assertion to a fully autonomous, self-correcting
end-to-end QA agent. This also explains why this review's four
research questions (landscape/taxonomy, autonomy level, empirical
evidence, and risks/open problems) are each phrased to cut across
rather than within a single activity: RQ3, in particular, requires
assessing the rigor and reproducibility of reported effectiveness
evidence (benchmark leakage, data contamination, generalizability)
across the whole corpus, a question none of the ten comparison
reviews poses as a dedicated, corpus-wide synthesis target, even
though several note isolated instances of weak or unverifiable
evidence within their own narrower scopes. In short, no prior survey or SLR identified
during screening provides a systematic, cross-granularity synthesis of
AI-based software testing organized around autonomy and its attendant
safety, reliability, and governance consequences; this review is
intended to fill precisely that gap, and the remaining sections
develop the taxonomy (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), present the
synthesized evidence per category (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and
discuss cross-cutting implications (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) that
follow from this positioning.
