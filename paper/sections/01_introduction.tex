\IEEEPARstart{S}{oftware} testing remains one of the most resource-intensive
activities in the software development lifecycle, commonly consuming
30--50\% of overall project effort~\cite{placeholder2024}. Traditional
testing techniques -- manual test case design, coverage-driven unit
testing, and scripted regression suites -- are increasingly challenged
by the scale, complexity, and release velocity of modern software
systems, including microservices, machine-learning-enabled components,
and continuously deployed cloud-native applications.

In response, a new generation of testing approaches has emerged,
leveraging artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML),
large language models (LLMs), self-adaptive test oracles, and
autonomous exploratory agents. These approaches promise to reduce
manual effort, improve defect detection, and adapt testing strategies
dynamically. However, the research landscape is fragmented: studies
differ in terminology (e.g., ``intelligent testing,'' ``autonomous
testing,'' ``AI-augmented testing''), evaluation benchmarks, and
maturity levels, making it difficult for researchers and practitioners
to assess the state of the art.

\subsection{Motivation}
The rapid adoption of LLMs in software testing has given rise to a new
generation of agentic, self-adaptive, and autonomous quality-engineering
techniques, ranging from multi-agent unit-test generation frameworks
that reconcile hallucinated oracles through panelist consensus, to
vision-driven GUI agents that detect non-crash functional bugs
invisible to crash-based testing, to self-healing locators and
flaky-test triage systems deployed at industrial scale. Yet this
progress is uneven and often methodologically fragile: independent
replications have shown that headline accuracy claims for neural
test-oracle generation and flaky-test classifiers can be substantially
overstated once data leakage and unrealistic class balance are
corrected, while several studies suggest that simple non-agentic
pipelines can match complex autonomous agents at a fraction of the
cost. Persistent challenges -- unreliable test oracles, limited
generalization beyond Java/Python and small-to-medium benchmarks,
unmeasured fault-detection effectiveness beneath coverage metrics,
unresolved context-management strategies for large codebases, and
emerging security vulnerabilities such as prompt injection against
coding agents -- indicate that the field lacks a systematic,
cross-cutting synthesis of what agentic AI testing techniques reliably
deliver versus where genuine autonomy remains aspirational. This
review addresses that gap (see
\texttt{literature-review/priority-papers-key-points.md} for the
per-study evidence underlying this motivation).

\subsection{Problem Statement}
No comprehensive, up-to-date systematic review consolidates these
next-generation software testing techniques under a unified
classification framework with rigorous methodological screening.

\subsection{Research Questions}
This review addresses the following research questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} What next-generation software testing techniques
        have been proposed in the literature, and how can they be
        classified?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} What levels of automation and autonomy do these
        techniques achieve across the test life cycle (design,
        generation, execution, oracle, maintenance)?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} What empirical evidence exists regarding the
        effectiveness, efficiency, and industrial adoption of these
        techniques, and how rigorous/reproducible is that evidence
        (e.g., benchmark leakage, generalizability beyond the
        originally reported settings)?
  \item \textbf{RQ4:} What are the open challenges and research gaps
        for next-generation software testing, including test-oracle
        reliability, cross-language/cross-scale generalization,
        context management for large codebases, and the security and
        trustworthiness of autonomous testing agents?
\end{itemize}
