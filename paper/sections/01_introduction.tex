Software testing remains one of the most resource-intensive
activities in the software development lifecycle, commonly consuming
30--50\% of overall project effort~\cite{bluemke2021}. Traditional
testing techniques -- manual test case design, coverage-driven unit
testing, and scripted regression suites -- are increasingly challenged
by the scale, complexity, and release velocity of modern software
systems, including microservices, machine-learning-enabled components,
and continuously deployed cloud-native applications. As systems grow
in size and interdependency, the cost of writing, executing, and
maintaining tests grows with them, and conventional automation --
record-and-replay scripts, parameterized unit tests, coverage-driven
fuzzers -- struggles to keep pace with the rate at which application
interfaces, business logic, and deployment environments change.

In response, a new generation of testing approaches has emerged,
leveraging artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML),
large language models (LLMs), self-adaptive test oracles, and
autonomous exploratory agents. These approaches promise to reduce
manual effort, improve defect detection, and adapt testing strategies
dynamically. However, the research landscape is fragmented: studies
differ in terminology (e.g., ``intelligent testing,'' ``autonomous
testing,'' ``AI-augmented testing''), evaluation benchmarks, and
maturity levels, making it difficult for researchers and practitioners
to assess the state of the art. This fragmentation is not merely
terminological. It reflects genuinely different design philosophies:
some systems retrofit LLMs as a smarter code-completion layer bolted
onto an otherwise conventional testing pipeline, while others treat
the LLM as the orchestrating controller of a multi-step, tool-using
agent that plans, executes, observes, and revises its own test
artifacts with little human supervision. Distinguishing between these
architectures, and establishing what empirical evidence actually
supports each, is a central motivation for this review.

\subsection{Motivation}
The rapid adoption of LLMs in software testing has given rise to a new
generation of agentic, self-adaptive, and autonomous quality-engineering
techniques, ranging from multi-agent unit-test generation frameworks
that reconcile hallucinated oracles through panelist consensus, to
vision-driven GUI agents that detect non-crash functional bugs
invisible to crash-based testing, to self-healing locators and
flaky-test triage systems deployed at industrial scale. Yet this
progress is uneven and often methodologically fragile: independent
replications have shown that headline accuracy claims for neural
test-oracle generation and flaky-test classifiers can be substantially
overstated once data leakage and unrealistic class balance are
corrected, while several studies suggest that simple non-agentic
pipelines can match complex autonomous agents at a fraction of the
cost. Persistent challenges -- unreliable test oracles, limited
generalization beyond Java/Python and small-to-medium benchmarks,
unmeasured fault-detection effectiveness beneath coverage metrics,
unresolved context-management strategies for large codebases, and
emerging security vulnerabilities such as prompt injection against
coding agents -- indicate that the field lacks a systematic,
cross-cutting synthesis of what agentic AI testing techniques reliably
deliver versus where genuine autonomy remains aspirational. This
review addresses that gap, drawing on the per-study evidence
synthesized in the methodology and results that follow.

Beyond the question of \emph{what works}, a second and increasingly
urgent motivation concerns \emph{what could go wrong}. As testing
agents gain the authority to generate, execute, and even autonomously
``heal'' failing tests, the field inherits an entirely new category of
risk that classical test-automation research never had to confront:
agents that test code can themselves be attacked (prompt injection,
sandbox escape, supply-chain poisoning of tool outputs), agents that
heal tests can silently suppress genuine regressions if the healing
criterion is miscalibrated, and agents that judge correctness via an
LLM-based oracle risk a circularity problem when the same family of
model that wrote the code under test is also asked to validate it. We
refer to this cluster of concerns collectively as \emph{quality
engineering safety}, and treat it as a first-class object of study in
this review rather than an afterthought, because it determines whether
autonomous testing can be trusted with escalating levels of
unsupervised operation (see Sections~\ref{sec:results}
and~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Problem Statement}
No comprehensive, up-to-date systematic review consolidates these
next-generation software testing techniques under a unified
classification framework with rigorous methodological screening.
Existing surveys (discussed in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:related-work}) tend to focus narrowly on a single
technique family -- for example, LLM-based unit-test generation, or
mutation testing -- and were largely conducted before the emergence of
tool-using, multi-step agentic architectures and before the quality
engineering safety concerns outlined above had been documented in the
literature. Consequently, researchers entering this space lack a
single reference point that (a) classifies the full breadth of
agentic and self-adaptive testing techniques, (b) critically appraises
the strength of the empirical evidence behind them, including where
that evidence has since been contested or overturned, and (c)
catalogs the safety, security, and governance problems that must be
solved before autonomous testing can be deployed with reduced human
oversight.

\subsection{Research Questions}
This review addresses the following research questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} What next-generation software testing techniques
        have been proposed in the literature, and how can they be
        classified?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} What levels of automation and autonomy do these
        techniques achieve across the test life cycle (design,
        generation, execution, oracle, maintenance)?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} What empirical evidence exists regarding the
        effectiveness, efficiency, and industrial adoption of these
        techniques, and how rigorous/reproducible is that evidence
        (e.g., benchmark leakage, generalizability beyond the
        originally reported settings)?
  \item \textbf{RQ4:} What are the open challenges and research gaps
        for next-generation software testing, including test-oracle
        reliability, cross-language/cross-scale generalization,
        context management for large codebases, and the security and
        trustworthiness of autonomous testing agents?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}
This review makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A systematic, reproducible literature review protocol
        (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), following the Kitchenham
        and PRISMA guidelines~\cite{kitchenham2007,page2021prisma},
        covering primary studies published between 2018 and 2026.
  \item An 18-category taxonomy of next-generation software testing
        techniques derived from 149 primary studies, spanning unit
        test generation, multi-agent/agentic test generation, GUI and
        mobile testing agents, API testing, fuzzing, mutation testing,
        flaky-test triage and self-healing automation, test oracles,
        benchmarks and contamination, human factors, and the security
        of testing agents themselves.
  \item A synthesis of empirical evidence on effectiveness and
        adoption barriers, including a critical appraisal of claims
        that do not survive leakage-free or cost-normalized
        re-evaluation.
  \item A dedicated synthesis of \emph{quality engineering safety}
        concerns -- oracle circularity, self-healing stability, agent
        security, and governance -- together with four candidate
        safety mechanisms proposed as starting points for future
        empirical validation (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
  \item A forward-looking research agenda of ten open problems
        (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}
