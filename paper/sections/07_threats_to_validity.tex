% Threats to Validity
%
% Standard SLR validity dimensions: construct, internal, external,
% conclusion (following Wohlin et al. and Kitchenham and Charters
% ~\cite{kitchenham2007}).
\subsection{Construct Validity}
Construct validity concerns whether the search and classification
process actually captured the concept of interest -- ``next-generation,
agentic, and self-adaptive software testing'' -- rather than a biased
or incomplete proxy for it. Three specific risks apply here. First,
terminology in this field is unusually unstable: the same underlying
idea is variously described as ``agentic AI,'' ``autonomous testing,''
``LLM-based testing,'' ``self-healing automation,'' or simply
``AI-augmented QA'' across different venues and years, so a search
string tuned to one vocabulary can silently miss studies that use
another. We mitigated this by iteratively expanding the search string
set across multiple rounds (documented in the supplementary search protocol)
and by snowballing from the reference lists and citing works of early seed papers, but
residual vocabulary drift cannot be fully ruled out, particularly for
very recent preprints that coin new terms before community consensus
forms. Second, the inclusion/exclusion criteria (detailed in
Section~\ref{subsec:study-selection}) necessarily
encode subjective judgment calls about what counts as ``agentic''
versus merely ``automated,'' and about the evidentiary bar for grey
literature (industry reports, preprints, and
practitioner studies), several of which were deliberately retained in
this corpus because they are presently the only public source of
evidence on specific industrial deployments (e.g., self-healing test
automation at scale); this is a conscious trade-off between coverage
and rigor, explicitly flagged in the study categorization rather than silently
excluded. Third, the taxonomy itself (18 categories) was constructed
inductively from the corpus rather than derived from a pre-registered
classification scheme, which means category boundaries reflect our
interpretive choices and a different research team might legitimately
draw them differently, though the underlying studies and their
assigned categories are openly documented in the companion replication dataset
for independent re-classification.

\subsection{Internal Validity}
Internal validity concerns whether the screening, quality-assessment,
and data-extraction process reliably produced the reported corpus and
findings, independent of who performed the work. The principal threat
is reviewer bias during title/abstract and full-text screening:
decisions about whether a borderline paper satisfies the inclusion
criteria were recorded during the title/abstract and full-text screening phases,
with representative primary studies undergoing multi-reviewer reconciliation;
this is an explicit, acknowledged limitation, and the
corresponding findings in Section~\ref{sec:results} and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} are presented with appropriate methodological caution.
For data extraction, structured forms were applied to reduce
inconsistency, but qualitative extraction in a rapidly evolving field
is inherently susceptible to categorization variance;
we mitigated this through systematic cross-checking against the five-point
quality checklist. A second internal-validity
threat is confirmation bias in the synthesis itself: because several
of the review's motivating observations (e.g., that some headline
accuracy claims do not survive leakage-corrected re-evaluation) align
with the authors' prior expectations about the field, we took care to
report both supporting and disconfirming evidence per claim and to
cite the specific re-evaluation studies rather than asserting the
critique unsupported, but the possibility of selective emphasis cannot
be entirely excluded without independent replication of the synthesis.

\subsection{External Validity}
External validity concerns the extent to which the review's findings
generalize beyond the specific studies, venues, databases, and time
window sampled. The corpus spans 2018--2026 and draws on leading
peer-reviewed venues (ACM, IEEE, Springer, Elsevier) alongside timely
preprints; this is appropriate for a fast-moving field where the
publication lag of traditional venues means the most relevant recent
work often appears first as a preprint, but it also
means that a fraction of the newest literature has not yet been through
extended journal revision cycles, and some conclusions
drawn from that work may be refined as replication studies emerge.
Programming-language and domain coverage is
also skewed: the majority of primary studies target Java and Python
codebases and web/REST or mobile-GUI application domains, so findings
about technique effectiveness may not generalize to other ecosystems
(e.g., embedded/real-time systems, systems languages such as C/C++/Rust,
or legacy mainframe codebases) that are comparatively under-studied in
this literature. Finally, because the search was conducted using a specific
set of digital libraries and search strings,
studies indexed only in venues or repositories outside that search
scope (for example, regional non-English venues, internal corporate
technical reports, or patent filings) are systematically absent from
the corpus, regardless of their potential relevance.

\subsection{Conclusion Validity}
Conclusion validity concerns whether the synthesis and interpretation
drawn from the extracted data are reliable and would be reproduced by
an independent team working from the same corpus. To support this, all
underlying artifacts -- the search log, the inclusion/exclusion
decisions, the extraction records, the categorized taxonomy,
and the full bibliography -- are openly available in the companion
replication package, which allows external
verification of every category count, publication-trend statistic, and
citation claim reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The main residual
threat is that several quantitative claims in this review (e.g.,
category and year-distribution counts) are descriptive statistics over
a purposively constructed, criteria-filtered sample rather than a
random sample of all possible testing literature, so
they should be interpreted as characterizing \emph{this reviewed
corpus} rather than as unbiased population-level estimates of the
field as a whole. Similarly, the four quality-engineering-safety
mechanisms proposed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} (TBTA, PPOV,
MG-DSV, and ATQSP) are explicitly framed as literature-synthesis-derived
proposals, not empirically validated solutions; no claim of
effectiveness is made for them, and their inclusion should not be
read as evidence that they outperform alternative designs not yet
conceived.
